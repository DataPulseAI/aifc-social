# 07. The relatable post

Written 11 October 2026. The production prompt for posts where a recognisable office moment
carries a usable AI tip. Governed by `docs/01` (voice), `docs/29` (cards) and the card law in the
shared brief. Where this file and those disagree, they win.

---

## 1. Aim, reader moment, metric

**Aim.** A post a manager sends to the person at the next desk with "this is us", and that
still teaches one thing they can do today. The joke is how the lesson gets in, not a garnish
on top of it.

**The reader moment.** Monday morning or Friday afternoon, phone in hand between jobs. The
owner of a 30-person builders' merchant, a practice manager at a dental surgery, an office
manager at an estate agent. They have tried ChatGPT once, got something bland back and
quietly decided it was overrated. They are not going to read a guide. They will stop for
something that describes their own week a bit too accurately.

**The metric, in order.**

1. **Reposts and sends.** The only mechanism that reaches people who do not follow a page
   with a few hundred readers (`docs/23` section 1). Record reposts from Buffer; sends are not
   exposed, so do not invent them.
2. **Tags.** Comments that name a colleague. Count them by hand in the weekly pass: a comment
   containing a tagged person counts once.
3. **Comments.** Any substantive comment. "Ha" counts for nothing in our read even if
   LinkedIn counts it.

Impressions and reactions are recorded but do not decide whether the format survives.

**Why this style exists.** The owner's verdict was that the posts are not catchy and the
cards give no immediate value. The relatable post answers the first half; the card law answers
the second. Our own study already points here: the single highest multiple in
`references/winners/index.md` about companies adopting AI badly is Alex Banks' agent-workflow
satire (7.0x his median, 1,414 reactions), and Heather Murray's 114-character joke about AI
photos ran at 2.4x hers. Both were funny about the reader's own world. Neither needed a meme.

## 2. What works

### Our own references

- `references/winners/index.md`: Banks 7.0x for satire of the reader's own industry ("replace
  the team with agents, discover they stall, hire people back, call it human-in-the-loop").
  Heather Murray 2.4x for a self-aware opener about the "genuinely thrilled" format, and 2.4x
  for a one-line joke. Short is not punished.
- `references/creators/alex-banks.md`: "His agent-workflow satire is the highest-performing
  thing in this whole roster that is about companies adopting AI badly, and it is exactly our
  subject." UK, calm register, never asks for anything.
- `references/creators/heather-murray.md`: our closest competitor's top tier is a joke, a
  self-deprecating post and a rhyming ode. Humour from a training business is normal, not risky.
- `references/creators/hills-and-jain.md`: Anisha Jain's imagined Apple meeting transcript as

- `references/scoring.md`, jaindl-25-ways-to-identify-ai-slop: "the quoted-phrase-then-verdict
  pill. Quoting the thing the reader writes every week is the highest-yield opening move on the

  that line.
- `references/authoredup/index.md` item 9: naming three specific bad habits beats one general
  complaint. The register there (swearing) is not ours.
- `docs/23` section 3: the "entertain" pillar is "satire with a serious point" and "the
  overheard dialogue". This file is that pillar made repeatable.

### Outside evidence, 2024 to 2026

- [Swani, Gulas and Dinsmore, "To humor or not humor buyers? Evaluating the effective use of
  humor in B2B advertisements", Journal of Business Research vol. 200, 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v200y2025ics0148296325004552.html).
  Four experiments, 305 participants in total (per-study sizes not shown on the abstract page).
  Humour raised attitudes to the ad and to the brand, and through them the intention to look
  for more information. The benefit shrank when buyers saw the humour as **unrelated to the
  product**. This is the academic version of our main rule: the joke has to be about the
  thing we teach.
- [Kantar, "Time to get serious about humour in advertising", 15 August 2024](https://www.kantar.com/inspiration/advertising-media/time-to-get-serious-about-humour-in-advertising).
  Humour in ads fell from 53% to 34% of ads globally since 2000, and fell further in digital
  and social. Kantar says humour connected to the brand performs better in the short and long
  term. Kantar does not publish the sample behind either figure on this page. The commercial
  read: the feed is less funny than it was, so a dry joke stands out more.
- [Marketing Week, Giles Edwards, "Humour in B2B is a little like sneaking medicine on pizza",
  8 May 2026](https://www.marketingweek.com/humour-b2b-medicine-pizza/). An opinion column, not
  a study. Useful line: "The more seriously you take your audience, the less seriously you need
  to take yourself." It cites System1's finding that top-quartile "making people laugh" ads
  are expected to have five times the share-of-voice efficiency of bottom-quartile ones; we
  have not opened System1's own report, so do not repeat that figure in a post.
- [Marketing Week, Megan Tatum, "'Nothing to lose': How humour became B2B's weapon of
  choice", 24 September 2026](https://www.marketingweek.com/humour-b2b-cut-through/). Paywalled
  past the opening; confirms the trend, carries no figure we could read.

What we could not find: any 2025 or 2026 study with a published method measuring humour on
**organic LinkedIn posts** specifically. The evidence above is about advertising. Say so if
anyone asks why we believe this works; the honest basis is the ad research plus our own
creator study.

### Pages and creators that use humour well with business audiences

Formats described from [Abe the Agency, "15 best B2B brands on LinkedIn"](https://www.abetheagency.com/blog/top-b2b-brands-linkedin)
(undated; screenshots captured 28 February 2025), [The Drum, 8 April 2025](https://www.thedrum.com/news/workday-doubles-down-humor-b2b-and-it-s-working-rock-star)
and our own reference notes.

| Who | The format, concretely | Take | Refuse |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alex Banks** (UK creator) | One image, a plain-text "workflow" that describes the reader's industry doing AI badly, step by step, ending on the euphemism they would use ("call it human-in-the-loop"). Calm register, no ask | The satirical procedure: steps the reader recognises, last step is the punchline, the serious point is obvious without stating it | Nothing. Closest model we have |
| **Heather Murray** (UK AI trainer) | A one-line observation about AI under an image; self-aware openers about LinkedIn's own clichés | Short, self-deprecating about our own category, never about the reader | The personal-photo element; a company page has no face |
| **Chili Piper** (meeting scheduling software) | Self-deprecating posts, quizzes, trends used "just enough"; described as relatable without tipping into cringe | Self-deprecation about the vendor's own world; the quiz shape | Trend-riding. Our reader does not follow the trends |
| **ClickUp** (work software) | Scripted short office sketches with actors: the meeting that should have been an email, the colleague who never reads the brief | The recognisable office type, written as dialogue | Video production and actors; and any sketch where a character is a figure of ridicule |
| **Semrush** (marketing software) | Plain educational tips with a meme or joke folded into the same post | The tip and the joke in one unit | Meme templates that need internet culture to decode |
| **Workday** ("Rock Star" campaign) | Super Bowl ad: rock legends recoil as office workers are called "rock stars". Mocks corporate jargon, not workers | The target is the jargon, which the reader also dislikes | Celebrity and budget, obviously |

**What we take.** The satirical procedure. The overheard line in quotes. The "what you typed
vs what you meant" pair. The office type everyone has worked with, described warmly. Jokes
whose target is jargon, hype, the AI tool itself, or us.

**What we refuse.**

- Meme templates and internet references (distracted boyfriend, "POV:", "nobody: / me:",
  "it's giving", Drake panels). A 55-year-old owner of a builders' merchant should never need
  a teenager to explain a post.
- Sarcasm aimed at the reader ("Still typing 'write me an email'? Embarrassing.").
- Jokes at staff's expense: the lazy junior, the useless receptionist, the technophobe in
  accounts. The reader employs those people and likes most of them.
- Jokes about age, gender, accents, regions, or anyone's competence with technology as a
  personal trait.
- Doom humour ("AI will take your job, lol"). Our stance is the calm thing in a loud room.
- Swearing, even asterisked.
- Anything where the laugh lands and there is nothing to do afterwards.

## 3. The production prompt

```text
You are writing one relatable post for the AI for Companies LinkedIn page. The reader owns or
manages a UK business of 5 to 250 staff (dental practice, estate agent, accountant, builders'
merchant, recruitment agency, cafe, solicitor, charity, trades). They have heard of ChatGPT,
may have tried it once, may pay for Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace. They are not technical
and must never feel stupid, lectured or sold to.

INPUTS
- tip: one usable AI action (the words to type, what to attach, or one check to run). Required.
  If you do not have one, stop. No tip, no post.
- tool_context: which assistants it works in. Default "ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever
  you have".
- sector (optional): a sector to set the moment in.
- source (optional): any figure must come from a document opened in this run, with publisher
  and date. Relatable posts rarely need a figure. Do not reach for one.

STEP 1. FIND THE RECOGNISABLE MOMENT
Write down five candidate moments from an ordinary UK working week that the tip fixes. Each
must be:
- physical and specific: a time, a place, an object, a phrase people say out loud
  ("Friday, 4.52pm", "the 63-email thread", "Has anyone actually read the handbook?");
- shared across sectors, or true of the named sector to anyone who works in it;
- about a situation, never about a type of person being bad at their job.
Pick the one a reader would describe to a colleague in their own words. If you would need to
explain why it is funny, discard it.

STEP 2. THE JOKE DELIVERS THE LESSON (the test)
State, in one line each:
  a) The joke: what is funny.
  b) The lesson: what the reader does differently.
  c) The link: why getting the joke means getting the lesson.
Pass only if (c) is true: the funny part and the useful part are the same thing. "What you
typed vs what you meant" passes, because the gap between the two IS the lesson. A joke about
the office printer followed by an unrelated prompt fails.
Second test: cover the joke. Is the card still usable? It must be. Cover the tip. Is it still
funny? It may be, but then it is a joke post and does not run.

STEP 3. REGISTER
Dry, British, warm. Understatement over exaggeration. The target of the joke is, in order of
preference: the situation, the jargon, the AI tool, ourselves. Never the reader, never their
staff, never a group of people. Read the post as the owner of a builders' merchant aged 55
would. If any line needs internet culture to decode, or could make them feel caught out, cut it.

STEP 4. CHOOSE THE FORMAT (pick one)
F1 What you typed / what you meant (beforeafter). Before: the short prompt people actually
   type. After: the same request with the context they had in their head. Title names the gap.
   e.g. before "Write a reply to this complaint." / after "Reply to this complaint from a
   customer whose delivery was 3 days late. Apologise once, give the new date, offer free
   delivery next time, under 120 words, no jargon."
F2 What most people type / what works (beforeafter). Same shape, framed as the common habit,
   never as a mistake. Labels "What we all type" and "What gets a usable answer".
F3 The keen new starter (statement or cheatsheet). AI is like a keen new starter: quick,
   confident, eager to please, and occasionally makes things up. Every tip is something you
   would also do with a new starter: give them the background, show an example, check their
   numbers. e.g. statement "Brief it like a new starter on their first morning: what the job
   is, who it's for, and what good looks like."
F4 The office phrase translator (cheatsheet). Label: a phrase everyone in an office says, in
   quotes. Value: the words to type that deal with it. e.g. "I'll write the minutes later" ->
   "Turn these notes into a table: action, owner, deadline. Flag anything with no owner."
F5 The week, by the clock (cheatsheet). Labels are times and days ("Monday, 9.04am",
   "Friday, 4.52pm"); each value is the prompt for that moment. The rhythm of the week is
   the joke, the prompts are the value.
F6 The honest job advert (cheatsheet). "Wanted: assistant. Good at / not good at." Labels such
   as "Hire it for", "Never trust it with", "Always check". Values are concrete tasks and
   checks. Teaches what AI is for in the shape of something everyone has read.
F7 Nobody has ever (statement). An observation everyone recognises that implies the tip. e.g.
   "Nobody has ever read the whole staff handbook. Paste it in and ask the one question you
   actually have." The second sentence is the tip, in the statement itself.
F8 The satirical procedure (cheatsheet). Banks' shape, ours: the steps a business really
   goes through ("Buy 20 licences. Send one email. Wait for magic."), labelled 1 to 4, then
   the last item is the fix. Use at most once a month; it is the easiest to tip into mockery.

STEP 5. THE CAPTION
- 300 to 800 characters. Hook inside 140 characters: the moment itself, stated flat.
  Never a question first.
- Paragraph 1: the moment. Paragraph 2: the tip in full, so the caption works without the card.
  Paragraph 3 (optional): one line of why it works, or the check to run. Then stop.
- Optional close: a substantive question only a real reader can answer ("Which one is your
  Monday?" fails; "What's the phrase your team says that should be a prompt?" passes once a
  fortnight at most). Never "tag someone", "share with your team", "agree?".
- If the tip involves personal data, staff, customers or contracts, add the plain check
  ("Take the names out first.") and, for UK GDPR, employment or contracts, end with:
  This is not legal advice.

STEP 6. THE CARD (one of these three JSON shapes exactly)
beforeafter:
{"template":"beforeafter","title":"","before":{"label":"","text":""},"after":{"label":"","text":""}}
statement:
{"template":"statement","text":"","sub":""}
cheatsheet:
{"template":"cheatsheet","title":"","items":[{"label":"","value":""}],"source":""}
Limits: title up to 12 words; cheatsheet 3 to 6 items; label 2 to 5 words; value up to 22
words and must be the usable thing (words to type, the check to run), never a teaser.
The card must be useful to someone who never reads the caption. The joke may live in the
title or labels; the value fields carry the lesson. A statement card's "sub" carries the tip
whenever the text is the observation.

ILLUSTRATIONS
Default: none. Do not request a generated image for a beforeafter, statement or cheatsheet
card. The only permitted use is the "strip" described in docs/prompts/07-relatable.md section
3a, and only when the batch explicitly allows a strip that week.

STEP 7. SELF-CHECK (all must be yes)
[ ] The tip is usable today with a tool the reader already has.
[ ] The joke and the lesson are the same thing (Step 2c written down).
[ ] Covering the joke leaves a usable card.
[ ] The target is a situation, jargon, the tool or us. Not the reader, staff or any group.
[ ] No meme template, internet slang, swearing or pop-culture reference.
[ ] A 55-year-old owner of a builders' merchant gets it in one read and would not feel daft.
[ ] Reading age about 12. No banned words (docs/01 section 4) and none of: agentic, LLM,
    tenant, roadmap, use case, workflow automation, leverage, transformation.
[ ] UK English. No em dashes, double hyphens, exclamation marks, emoji, hashtags, italics.
    "We" is the company, never "I".
[ ] No invented clients, quotes or results. Any example is clearly an example.
[ ] Any figure has a source opened this run, with publisher and date.
[ ] No engagement bait. Legal line present where required.
[ ] Card JSON within limits; every value is a thing to type or do.
```

### 3a. The strip: when a generated illustration earns its place

**Verdict: almost never.** Single illustrations on beforeafter, statement and cheatsheet cards
are refused outright. They fail card-law rule 2 by definition: a picture of a person at a desk
looking puzzled carries no information, and it trains readers that our images are decoration.

There is one narrow exception, run as an experiment, at most one relatable post in ten, and
only after the owner has approved a first test batch of three:

**The strip.** Three or four panels, each panel one step of a procedure, where the picture
shows something the words alone do not: which thing to attach, what the pile looks like
before and the one page after, where the paste goes. Every word on the strip (panel captions,
title) is set by our renderer in our typeface beneath each panel. The generated art contains
no text at all.

This needs a `strip` template that does not exist in the shared schema yet. Until it does,
no strip runs. The proposed shape, for the renderer agent to accept or reject:

```json
{"template":"strip","title":"","panels":[{"image":"","caption":""}],"source":""}
```

Strict conditions, all required:

1. **The cover test.** Cover each picture. If the caption alone carries everything, the picture
   goes and the post runs as a cheatsheet. Cover each caption. The picture must still show a
   distinguishable step.
2. **No text in the image.** Models garble letters. Reject any output with letters, numbers,
   pseudo-writing, readable screens or signage, and regenerate.
3. **No real people, logos, brands or products.** No recognisable laptops, phone makers,
   software interfaces, celebrity likenesses or real premises.
4. **Generic, respectful people.** Adults, ordinary work clothes, no caricature, no one
   portrayed as the butt of the joke. Vary age and appearance across a strip without making
   any of it the point.
5. **Two brand colours on stone.** Deep green #1f5a3e and charcoal #17211c on #f5f5f2,
   flat, so it sits inside the card system.
6. **Disclosed in the bank.** Record `image_source=generated` and the prompt used in the
   post's bank file.

The image prompt template, one call per panel, same seed and style line across the strip:

```text
Flat vector illustration, simple geometric shapes, two colours only: deep green #1f5a3e and
charcoal #17211c on a plain off-white #f5f5f2 background. Clean editorial style, thick even
lines, no gradients, no shadows, no texture.
Scene: [one ordinary UK small-business setting, e.g. the back office of a builders' merchant /
a dental practice reception / a cafe counter after closing].
Action: [the single step of this panel, as a physical action, e.g. "one adult drags a thick
stack of loose papers into a laptop" / "one adult holds up a single tidy sheet of paper"].
People: [one or two] generic adults in ordinary work clothes, simple faces, calm or mildly
amused expressions, no caricature.
Hard constraints: absolutely no text, letters, numbers, symbols, writing, signage or readable
screens anywhere; screens show plain blocks of colour only; no logos, no brands, no
recognisable products or real people. Square 1:1 composition, subject centred, generous
margin on all sides.
```

Reject and regenerate if any output fails a constraint. Two failed regenerations on the same
panel means the strip does not run; ship the cheatsheet instead.

## 4. Three worked examples

### Example 1. What you typed vs what you meant (F1, beforeafter)

Caption:

```text
"Write a reply to this complaint." Six words, and you wonder why it came back sounding like a bank.

Everything that makes a good reply was in your head, not in the box. Who the customer is, what went wrong, what you're offering, how long it should be.

Try it with the detail you'd give a colleague: "Reply to this complaint from a customer whose delivery was 3 days late. Apologise once, give the new date, offer free delivery on the next order, under 120 words, no jargon." Paste the complaint underneath.

Works the same in ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever you have.
```

Card:

```json
{"template":"beforeafter","title":"What you typed, and what you meant","before":{"label":"What you typed","text":"Write a reply to this complaint."},"after":{"label":"What you meant","text":"Reply to this complaint from a customer whose delivery was 3 days late. Apologise once, give the new date, offer free delivery next time. Under 120 words, no jargon."}}
```

Step 2 record: joke, the six-word prompt for a 60-word thought; lesson, put the context in;
link, the gap between the two boxes is the lesson. Note the after text runs past 22 words: it
is a beforeafter text field, not a cheatsheet value, but the renderer agent should confirm it
fits at 34px; if not, cut "no jargon".

### Example 2. The keen new starter (F3, statement)

Caption:

```text
AI is a keen new starter. Quick, confident, eager to please, and every so often it makes something up with a straight face.

You wouldn't hand a new starter a job with no background and sign off whatever came back. Same here. Tell it what the job is, who it's for, and what good looks like. Then check anything with a number, a name or a date in it before it leaves the building.

That's most of the training, in a sentence.
```

Card:

```json
{"template":"statement","text":"AI is a keen new starter: quick, confident, and now and then it makes things up.","sub":"Brief it like day one. Check every number, name and date."}
```

### Example 3. The office phrase translator (F4, cheatsheet)

Caption:

```text
Five things said in every office in the country, every week. Each one is a job your AI assistant will do in under a minute.

The words to type are on the card. Paste the notes, the thread, the policy or the sheet underneath, in ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever you have.

Take customers' and staff names out before you paste anything into a tool your business hasn't approved.
```

Card:

```json
{"template":"cheatsheet","title":"Five things every office says, and what to type instead","items":[
{"label":"\"I'll do the minutes later\"","value":"Turn these notes into a table: action, owner, deadline. Flag anything with no owner."},
{"label":"\"Can you just reword this?\"","value":"Rewrite this for an annoyed customer. Under 100 words, plain English, apologise once."},
{"label":"\"Has anyone read the policy?\"","value":"Summarise this policy in five bullet points a new starter would understand."},
{"label":"\"Where did we land on this?\"","value":"Read this email thread. What was decided, what's still open, and who's waiting on whom?"},
{"label":"\"Nobody gets this spreadsheet\"","value":"Explain what each column in this sheet does, in plain English, one line each."}],"source":""}
```

## 5. Before and after: 2026-09-25-stop-pasting-grid

**Before** (`bank/posts/2026-09-25-stop-pasting-grid.md`, `cards/2026-09-25-stop-pasting-grid.png`).
Headline "Eight things to stop pasting into AI at work". A 2x4 grid of boxed cells, four type
sizes, uppercase titles, notes that half-explain ("The NDA does not mention AI. That is the
problem, not the defence."). The content is good and the frame is a compliance poster: it
tells the reader off before it helps them, and nothing in it is something you would forward
with "this is us".

What it gets right and we keep: the categories, the NCSC framing that staff go round slow
approved routes, and the instruction to name the approved tool alongside the ban.

**The relatable angle.** Everyone has had a temp or a new starter. Nobody hands them the
client contracts on their first morning. That test is instantly understood, is a little bit
funny, and is the actual rule.

**After.** Caption:

```text
Would you hand it to a temp on their first morning, before they've signed anything? Then don't paste it into an AI tool your business hasn't approved.

That one question covers most of the list. Client contracts, staff records, CVs with home addresses, card and bank details, anything under an NDA. A temp would get the job without the details, and so can the AI.

The National Cyber Security Centre made the point on 7 September 2026: people use unapproved tools because the approved route is slower or missing, and shadow AI is "unlikely to disappear completely". So name the tool people should use, in the same breath as the rule.

This is not legal advice.

https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/the-hidden-risks-of-shadow-ai
```

Card:

```json
{"template":"cheatsheet","title":"Would you hand it to a temp on day one? If not, don't paste it.","items":[
{"label":"Client contracts","value":"Paste the clause you need help with, with the client's name and the rates taken out."},
{"label":"Staff records","value":"Never names with pay, absence or reviews. Describe the situation without the person."},
{"label":"CVs","value":"Delete the name, address and date of birth first. The skills are what you need read."},
{"label":"Card and bank details","value":"Never. There's no tool, plan or setting where this is fine."},
{"label":"Anything under an NDA","value":"Ask whoever owns the NDA before it goes into any AI tool."},
{"label":"Everything else","value":"Fine, in the tool your business has approved. Make sure everyone knows which one that is."}],"source":"NCSC, The hidden risks of shadow AI, 7 September 2026"}
```

Why it is better: the title is the test, so the card is usable with no caption; each value
says what to do instead, not just what not to do; the last row gives permission, which is what
stops the slow-route problem the NCSC describes. Six rows instead of eight, so it fills the
canvas at body size rather than shrinking to fit boxes.

## 6. Fail list: eight ways this goes cringe or alienating

| # | Failure | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The joke and the tip are strangers** | A gag about the office printer, then an unrelated prompt | Rewrite until Step 2c is true, or run the tip as a plain cheatsheet |
| 2 | **Mocking the reader** | "Still typing 'write me an email'? We need to talk." | Make the target the habit everyone shares, in "we all" form: "What we all type" |
| 3 | **Staff as punchline** | The useless junior, the receptionist who "can't work the computer" | Target the situation (the 63-email thread), never the person in it |
| 4 | **Meme literacy required** | "POV:", "nobody: / me:", Drake panels, "it's giving" | Say the moment in plain words a 55-year-old would use in the yard |
| 5 | **Explaining the joke** | "See what we did there?", "Funny, but true" | Delete the explanation. If it needs one, it was not recognisable enough; pick another moment |
| 6 | **Decorative illustration** | A generated cartoon of a confused man at a laptop above the tip | Remove it. Cover test: it carried nothing. Only the gated strip in 3a may use art |
| 7 | **Forced zaniness** | Puns stacked three deep, a mascot, "AI-mazing", exclamation marks | One dry line of understatement. If it would not survive being said aloud in a meeting, cut it |
| 8 | **Doom or snark about AI** | "AI will do your job, enjoy your redundancy", "AI is useless lol" | Keep the calm stance: the tool is fine, it needs briefing and checking, like anyone new |
