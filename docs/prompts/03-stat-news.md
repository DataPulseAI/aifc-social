# 03. The stat or news post

Written 11 October 2026. One fresh, sourced fact that matters to a UK small business owner, made immediately useful. This is the "wide" band in `docs/22` when the number sounds wrong and has a named publisher behind it, and the "do this Monday" band when it does not. Where this disagrees with `docs/01` (voice) or `docs/10` (the sourcing gate), those win.

Every figure quoted in this file was opened on 11 October 2026 at the URL given next to it.

---

## 1. Aim, reader moment, metric

**Aim.** The reader sees one number, knows in a second why it is about their business, and leaves with one thing to do before Friday. If the reader has to work out why it concerns them, the post has failed, however good the number.

**The reader moment.** The owner of a 20-person dental practice, estate agency or accountancy, on a phone, between a client and a call, thumb moving. They give the first line about one second. They are not technical, slightly wary of AI, and tired of being told they are behind. What stops them is a number about firms like theirs, in words they would use, that does not make them feel stupid.

**The metric, in order.**

1. **Reposts and sends.** The stat post is the one a reader forwards to a business partner or office manager with "we should do this". `docs/23` section 1: what gets forwarded is something a colleague can use, and for a page with very few followers, reposts are the only way to reach people who do not follow us. A stat post with zero sends has informed and not travelled.
2. **Dwell.** A number makes people stop and check they read it right (`docs/08` rule 3). The body has to repay the stop with the translation and the action, or dwell is three seconds.
3. **Comments, last.** Stat posts rarely start threads (`docs/23`: comments cluster on people, positions and corrections). Do not chase them with a question close. A correction of a belief the reader holds ("AI is cutting jobs") is the exception and may draw replies on its own.

**Why this style exists at all.** Our own numbers, small as they are: number-first hooks ran 5.98% mean engagement over 4 posts in the week to 21 September, and the best post of that week was a stat-led NCSC post (`docs/21` section 2). The outside evidence points the same way (section 2 below). The problem has never been the number. It has been choosing numbers about other people.

## 2. What works

### Our references

- **`docs/08` section 2, the scoring gate.** Question 1 (would a manager read this out to the person next to them) is the gate. Question 3 (is there a number that sounds wrong) is what this style lives on. Section 2 also warns that the stat card is a saves format, not a reach format, unless the number is surprising. That is why this style requires a translation and an action: a bare stat card is the format `docs/08` told us to stop leading with.
- **`docs/09`, the headline rules.** A subject does something. One surprising number. Give away the whole thing, no colons, no "here's why". The model line is "35% of UK firms use AI. The average one uses it for 1.6 things." The negative rule (no "rollout", "adoption", "framework") applies to every hook here.
- **`docs/22` section 2.** `stat-in-context` is wide when the number sounds wrong and a named publisher stands behind it, filler when the number is unsurprising. The construction 13% post went back to the bank as "a good number waiting for a slot".
- **`docs/03`, the sources.** The calendared UK sources (ONS, DSIT, NCSC, ICO, Enterprise Nation) are where this style should draw from. Its dead-URL list saves a wasted run. Its Deloitte trap (old content presented as current) is the reason every source date is checked on the document itself.
- **`docs/10`, the sourcing gate.** Opened during the run, or it does not appear. The counting rule. "Write the smaller true thing" when a detail cannot be confirmed.
- **`docs/13` section 1.** No figure repeats within 21 days; no source supplies more than two of the last ten posts.
- **`references/winners/index.md`.** Chris Donnelly's 5.8x post opens on a number that sounds wrong and turns it into permission ("the average millionaire is 61"). Jérémy Grandillon's 4.2x is an original count nobody else had. Both say: the number earns the stop, the turn that follows earns the share.
- **`references/creators/alex-banks.md`.** His news posts work when they carry one sentence of interpretation the reader could not have written. A bare announcement does not travel, even with a huge audience behind it.
- **`references/top-content/index.md`.** 72 of the 100 posts LinkedIn itself curated open with a flat statement. The statement is the workhorse.
- **`references/claims.md` and `references/authoredup/index.md`.** Hooks of 40 characters or fewer earn the most; question openers come last; a closing question moves the median by almost nothing.

### Outside evidence, 2025 to 2026

- [MagicPost, LinkedIn statistics 2026: 32 first-party numbers from 1.2M posts, last updated 5 June 2026](https://magicpost.in/blog/linkedin-statistics). 1,207,797 posts from personal profiles, not company pages. "Number-led hooks overperform: 35 median likes versus 26, a 35% lift" (1,179,960 posts). Question hooks: 19 median likes against 29 for statement openers. Median post 1,011 characters, and likes rise with length (19 under 25 words, 47 at 400+). Images 34 median likes against text 16. This is the "1.18 million post study" `docs/01` cites, now opened.
- [AuthoredUp, LinkedIn hook examples, updated 11 August 2026](https://authoredup.com/blog/linkedin-hook-examples). 309,614 profile posts, December 2025 to May 2026. Hooks of 0 to 40 characters: 2.61% median engagement against 2.08% at 200+. "Results" hooks (a number about an outcome) 2.19%, below "statement" at 2.27%. Read with MagicPost: a number helps when it is stated flatly, not when it is a boast.
- [Ordinal, LinkedIn hooks, Jeffrey Zhao, 18 July 2026](https://www.tryordinal.com/blog/linkedin-hooks). Reports ConnectSafely's figure that hooks under 10 words beat longer ones by 40% on "see more" clicks; sample size not stated. It gives no data on number-led hooks.
- [Storykit, why micro-data beats big numbers in LinkedIn headlines, updated 8 May 2026](https://storykit.io/blog/why-micro-data-beats-big-numbers-in-linkedin-headlines). Claims specific figures drive 41% more clicks and that numbers in the first eight words work best. No sample size, method or period published, and it is vendor marketing. Soft; recorded so nobody cites it as hard.

All of these measure personal profiles. None measures company pages, and none separates "number about the reader" from "number about someone else". The evidence says numbers stop people. It says nothing about whether they travel. That part is our judgement, and it is the point of the relevance test below.

### Five accounts that make numbers and news must-read for non-specialists

1. **MoneySavingExpert (moneysavingexpert.com/news, opened 11 October 2026; their dashes replaced with commas below).** The best model for this style anywhere. It addresses the reader by their situation, then says what it means for them and what to do: "Halifax customer? Your online banking will be axed from 31 October, here's how to move to the Lloyds Bank app" (25 September 2026); "Base rate held AGAIN at 3.75%, here's what it means for you and when it might change" (22 September 2026); "Full new State Pension likely to rise to £13,000 a year next April" (15 September 2026). The number is translated into the reader's own money or date, and the action is in the headline.
2. **Visual Capitalist (visualcapitalist.com, opened 11 October 2026).** One chart, one plain headline that states the finding ("Stablecoin Card Spending Has Surged to $1 Billion a Month"), and a one or two sentence summary underneath. The picture carries the whole fact on its own, which is our card law.
3. **Morning Brew (morningbrew.com, opened 11 October 2026).** Business news for people who are not specialists. Of six headlines read, none carried a number: "Robots can't do all of Walmart's heavy lifting yet", "The most profitable US airline can't shake oil shocks". The figures sit in the body; the headline says what the figure means in everyday words.
4. **Alex Banks (LinkedIn, `references/creators/alex-banks.md`, read 21 September 2026).** UK, calm register, 40 posts read. His news posts that worked carry one sentence of interpretation the reader could not have written: "enterprises wouldn't touch Grok, so it bought the tool they already use" (592 reactions). He never asks for anything.
5. **The Rundown AI (therundown.ai, opened 11 October 2026).** An AI explainer for non-specialists. Each item is a category tag, a headline that names who did what ("Biohub expands AI cell research into a $1.8 billion effort", 9 October 2026), and a one or two sentence summary. Their stated job: "The stories shaping AI, and why they matter."

### What we take

- From MSE: name the reader's situation, put the number in their units (their money, their staff, their week), and put the action next to the number.
- From Visual Capitalist: the card states the finding so it works with no caption at all.
- From Morning Brew: the meaning can lead and the number can follow, when the meaning is the surprise.
- From Banks: one sentence of interpretation the reader could not have written. Only one.
- From Donnelly's 5.8x: a number that sounds wrong, turned into permission rather than fear.

### What we refuse

- MSE's capitals ("AGAIN"), dashes and exclamation register. Our voice rules stand.
- Morning Brew's wordplay headlines. Our reader is not here for puns, and they read as trying.
- The Rundown's diet of US frontier-lab news. Our reader runs a builders' merchant.
- The "Ranked:" and "Mapped:" colon prefix. `docs/09` rule 3: no colons in the hook.
- The four-surveys roundup shape, the borrowed-sector number, and the vendor-commissioned survey presented as fact (section 6).

## 3. The production prompt

Copy everything inside the fence. Fill the inputs block before running it.

```text
You are writing one LinkedIn post for the AI for Companies company page: a stat or news post. The page trains staff at UK small and medium businesses (5 to 250 staff) to use the AI tools they already have.

THE READER
The owner or manager of a UK firm of about 20 people: a dental practice, estate agency, accountancy, builders' merchant, recruitment agency, cafe, solicitors, charity or trade. Not technical. Has heard of ChatGPT, may have tried it, may pay for Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace. Slightly wary. Must never feel stupid, lectured, sold to or behind. Reading age about 12. Contractions are fine.

INPUTS (fill all of these from a document you opened in this run; leave nothing blank)
- source_url: the primary document itself (gov.uk, ons.gov.uk, ncsc.gov.uk, ico.org.uk, bankofengland.co.uk, fsb.org.uk, britishchambers.org.uk or similar), not a news story about it
- publisher: as shown on the document (e.g. "ONS"; "DSIT and DCMS")
- title: exact title of the document
- published: date as shown on the document, written as 8 October 2026
- figure_sentence: the exact sentence the number comes from, quoted
- population: exactly who the number describes (e.g. "UK businesses with 10 or more employees that use AI")
- unit: what the number counts (firms, staff, pounds, hours, postings)
- period: when it was measured (fieldwork dates or reference month)
- sample: number of responses, or the words "not published"
- caveats: anything the document itself says about quality (e.g. "official statistics in development")

THE SOURCING GATE (hard; stop if any fails)
1. You opened source_url in this run and read figure_sentence on the page. A figure recalled, seen in a newsletter or quoted by a news site does not count.
2. The publication date is on the document, and it is within 14 days for the news slot, or within 12 months for an evergreen slot. Older figures run only if no newer edition exists and the date is in the first two lines of the body.
3. You state no count you did not count. "Around a third" is fine only if the source says so.
4. The figure has not appeared on the page in the last 21 days, and this publisher has not supplied more than two of the last ten posts (run: node lib/freshness.mjs bank/ledger.csv).
5. A survey paid for by a company that sells AI does not run as the lead number. If used at all, the body says who paid for it.
If the gate fails, write "GATE FAILED: <reason>" and stop.

THE RELEVANCE TEST (one second)
Read the number to the owner of a 20-person firm. Do they think "that's about firms like mine" or "that's about my staff, my money, my risk, my week" within one second? It passes only if the population includes them (all UK businesses, small businesses, their size band, or their sector) or the number lands on something they own (staff, customers, cash, data, a deadline).
It fails if the population is another sector (cyber security firms, construction when they are a dental practice), large enterprises only, another country, or AI vendors. A failing number can still run only if you can state in one sentence why the reader's own firm is in the same position, and that sentence is true without a second source. If you need a second number to make it relevant, choose a different number.

TRANSLATE THE NUMBER INTO THEIR WORLD
Pick one translation and keep the unit honest:
- Per business: a share of firms becomes "line up four firms like yours: one has X". Only when the unit is firms.
- Per person: a share of staff becomes "in a team of 20, about N people". Only when the unit is people. Never turn a share of firms into a share of staff: "70% of firms say staff use AI" does not mean 70% of staff.
- Per week or per pound: a time or money figure becomes their week or their invoice. Only when the source gives time or money.
- By size: if the source breaks the number down by size, use the band nearest 20 staff and name it.
Round the way the source rounds or more cautiously ("about 1 in 4" for 24%). Never round up into a stronger claim. "Most" means over 50% in the source.

THE "SO WHAT, SO DO THIS" LINE
One sentence of meaning the reader could not have written themselves, marked as our read where it goes beyond the source ("our read is", "for a firm your size, the likely change is"). Then one action:
- Doable today or this week, in under 30 minutes, by the owner, with nothing to buy.
- Concrete nouns: who, what, where. "List the five jobs your office manager repeats every week" passes. "Review your AI strategy" fails.
- If it involves a tool: "ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever you have".
- Calm. Never "you're behind", "act now", "don't get left behind".

HOOK (first line, under 80 characters, number in the first six words where possible)
Use one of these formulas. Do not use the same formula as either of the last two posts.
1. The odds in your street: "Line up four UK firms trying AI. Three have nothing in place for the risks."
2. The fear, checked: "AI cut headcount at 6% of UK firms with 10+ staff that use it."
3. Your size band: "29% of UK firms with fewer than 10 staff use AI. At 250 or more, it's 51%."
4. The plain count: "808,000 UK businesses say they lack basic cyber skills."
5. The cautious go first: "7 in 10 UK cyber security firms say their staff use AI every day."
6. The situation address (MSE): "If you have an office manager, this ONS number is about them."
7. The flat finding: "Four in ten UK businesses had a cyber breach or attack last year."
8. The meaning first (Morning Brew): "UK firms using AI say admin roles are the most affected. Few have cut staff."
Hooks never open with a question, "Most people", "Everyone is", "Nobody talks about", or our name. No colon in the hook. Give the whole finding away.

CAPTION
- 750 to 1,100 characters including the source line. Count them.
- Order: hook; the source in plain words with date and who was asked (sample, or "the report doesn't say how many"); the translation; one sentence of meaning; the action, numbered if it has steps; stop.
- Paragraphs of one to three sentences, varied. No eight one-line paragraphs in a row.
- One number leads. At most two supporting figures, both from the same document. Never a second survey.
- Close with the action or a hard sentence. No question close, no "save this", no "follow", no "agree?".
- If anything touches UK GDPR, data, employment, jobs, headcount or contracts, the last line before the source is: This is not legal advice.

CITING IN THE BODY
- Name the publisher and date in plain words in the second paragraph: "That's from the ONS, published 8 October 2026."
- Last line, always: Source: <publisher>, <exact title>, <published>. <sample or "Sample size not published">. <caveat if any>. <source_url>
- One link only: the source. No link to our site in a stat post.

CARD JSON (stat template; beforeafter only when the source gives a then-and-now or a two-group comparison from the same document)
{"template":"stat","number":"","meaning":"","action":"","source":""}
- number: the figure exactly as the hook states it, at most 8 characters ("1 in 4", "6%", "808,000").
- meaning: who it describes and what it counts, up to 22 words, in the reader's words. The card must make sense with no caption.
- action: the same action as the caption, up to 22 words, starting with a verb. This is the usable thing; a card with an empty or vague action fails.
- source: "<publisher>, <published>", e.g. "ONS, 8 October 2026".
No eyebrow, no kicker, no "up from" in the footer, no slogan. Title and value only.
beforeafter form: {"template":"beforeafter","title":"","before":{"label":"","text":""},"after":{"label":"","text":""}}

HOUSE RULES
UK English. No em dashes, no double hyphens, no exclamation marks, no emoji, no hashtags, no italics. Dates as 8 October 2026. "We" is the company, "you" is the reader, never "I". No invented clients, results, quotes or case studies; illustrative examples say so ("say you run a dental practice"). Banned words: delve, leverage, utilise, robust, seamless, unlock, harness, elevate, supercharge, game changer, landscape, realm, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, vital, agentic, LLM, tenant, roadmap, use case, workflow automation, transformation.

SELF-CHECK (print each line with PASS or FAIL before the final post; fix every FAIL)
1. Every number in the caption and card appears in figure_sentence or elsewhere on the opened page. List each number with the sentence it came from.
2. Population and unit are stated, and the translation keeps the same unit.
3. Relevance: a 20-person firm owner gets it in one second. Say why in one line.
4. The hook is under 80 characters, has no colon, no question, and gives the whole finding.
5. The card alone gives the number, what it means and what to do.
6. The action is doable this week in under 30 minutes with nothing to buy.
7. Caption is 750 to 1,100 characters. State the count.
8. Source line present with date and sample (or "not published").
9. "This is not legal advice." present if data, GDPR, employment, jobs or contracts are touched.
10. No banned word, em dash, double hyphen, exclamation mark, hashtag, question close or engagement bait.
11. No claim of clients, delivery or "in our experience".

OUTPUT
1. Self-check results.
2. The caption, ready to paste.
3. The card JSON.
4. Ledger fields: source_url, source_publisher, source_date, hook_type (formula number), figure.
```

## 4. Two worked examples

Both built on figures opened on 11 October 2026.

### Example 1: the three-line AI rule

**Source.** [DSIT and DCMS, Cyber security breaches survey 2025/2026, 30 April 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/cyber-security-breaches-survey-20252026/cyber-security-breaches-survey-20252026). 2,112 UK businesses, random probability telephone and online survey, August to December 2025. The sentences used: "Around a third of businesses (31%) ... were either using AI (Artificial Intelligence), in the process of adopting it or actively considering using it." "Of this group, around a quarter of businesses (24%) ... reported having cyber security practices or processes in place to manage the risks from the use of AI (Artificial Intelligence) technology." The report does not publish how many businesses were in the AI group. The publication is six months old, so this runs in an evergreen slot, not the news slot, with the date in the second paragraph.

**Relevance.** Population is all UK businesses using or looking at AI: the reader is in it. Unit is firms, so the translation is per business.

**Caption** (1,074 characters):

```text
Only 24% of UK firms using or eyeing up AI have anything in place for the risks.

That's from the government's Cyber Security Breaches Survey, published 30 April 2026. It asked 2,112 UK businesses between August and December 2025. About a third were using AI, adopting it or considering it. Of those, 24% had practices or processes in place to manage the risks.

Line up four firms like yours that are trying AI. Three have nothing in place.

Our read: for a 20-person firm, the likely problem is someone pasting a client's details or a supplier's bank details into a free chatbot to tidy up an email.

Three lines, sent to everyone today, cover most of it:
1. Which tool we use: ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever we pay for.
2. What never goes in: client names, staff records, bank details, health information.
3. Who to ask if you're not sure.

This is not legal advice.

Source: DSIT and DCMS, Cyber security breaches survey 2025/2026, 30 April 2026. Size of the AI group not published. https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/cyber-security-breaches-survey-20252026
```

**Card:**

```json
{"template":"stat","number":"1 in 4","meaning":"UK firms using or considering AI that have anything in place to manage its risks.","action":"Send your team three lines today: which AI tool we use, what never goes in, who to ask.","source":"DSIT and DCMS, 30 April 2026"}
```

### Example 2: AI and the admin desk

**Source.** [ONS, Business insights and impact on the UK economy, 8 October 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/business/businessservices/bulletins/businessinsightsandimpactontheukeconomy/8october2026). Wave 165 of the Business Insights and Conditions Survey, live 21 September to 4 October 2026, 9,933 responses from a sample of 38,592 (25.7%). Official statistics in development. The sentences used: "In September 2026, of businesses with 10 or more employees that were currently using AI, 6% reported reduced headcount as a result." "Fewer than 1% reported that their headcount had increased." "38% reported administrative and clerical roles were the most affected." "The next highest proportion was for creative or design roles, at 24%, broadly stable compared with December 2025."

**Relevance.** Population is UK firms with 10 or more staff that use AI: a 20-person firm is in the middle of it. It corrects a fear the reader holds (AI means job cuts) and points at a named person in their own office. Unit is firms; the translation stays per business.

**Caption** (1,075 characters):

```text
AI cut headcount at 6% of UK firms with 10+ staff that use it.
Admin roles felt it most.

That's from the ONS, published 8 October 2026, from 9,933 businesses asked in September. Of firms with 10 or more staff that use AI, 6% said headcount fell as a result and fewer than 1% said it rose. 38% said admin and clerical roles were the most affected.

So for a firm your size, the likely change isn't fewer people. It's that whoever runs your admin is the first person whose week looks different.

Start there. This week, sit with them for 20 minutes and list the five jobs they repeat every week: chasing invoices, typing up notes, answering the same three emails. Pick one. Try it in ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever you have, and let them judge the result. They know the job.

This is not legal advice.

Source: ONS, Business insights and impact on the UK economy, 8 October 2026. 9,933 responses. Official statistics in development. https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/business/businessservices/bulletins/businessinsightsandimpactontheukeconomy/8october2026
```

**Card:**

```json
{"template":"stat","number":"6%","meaning":"of UK firms with 10+ staff using AI say it cut headcount. 38% say admin roles were most affected.","action":"Sit with whoever does your admin for 20 minutes. List five weekly jobs. Try one with AI.","source":"ONS, 8 October 2026"}
```

## 5. Before and after: 2026-10-06-cyber-firms-70

**Source re-checked on 11 October 2026.** [DSIT, Cyber security skills in the UK labour market 2026, 29 September 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cyber-security-skills-in-the-uk-labour-market-2026/cyber-security-skills-in-the-uk-labour-market-2026). The figure holds: "70% reporting that staff were using AI in their day-to-day work, up from 53% last year", among cyber security businesses. Base 230 cyber security businesses, fieldwork 8 August to 27 October 2025, covering calendar year 2025. The banked post's facts were correct. The post failed on relevance and on the card, not on sourcing.

**Before.** Hook: "70% of UK cyber security firms say their staff use AI in day-to-day work. Last year it was 53%." Card: eyebrow "THE SECTOR THAT WORRIES MOST", the number, a meaning line, the source, a wordmark and an "UP FROM 53% LAST YEAR" kicker; no action; a band of empty space under the source.

**Why it failed.**
- The population is 230 cyber security firms. A dental practice owner reads "cyber security firms" and scrolls: not me. The reason it concerns them arrives in paragraph three.
- The card has no action. Read alone, it is a fact about another industry.
- Four type sizes, an eyebrow, a footer kicker and dead space: everything the card law forbids.
- It ends on a link to our site, which a stat post should not carry.

**After.** The fix leads with the meaning the reader cares about (the most careful firms have let it in, so it is already in yours) and ends on the one action (1,050 characters). The number stays, the sector is named honestly, the translation stays per firm.

```text
7 in 10 UK cyber security firms say their staff now use AI every day.

These are the firms paid to keep other people's data safe. The government asked 230 of them, and 70% said their staff use AI in their day-to-day work, up from 53% the year before. That's from DSIT's cyber security skills report, published 29 September 2026.

Our read: if the most careful firms in the country use it daily, assume someone in your team already does too. It arrives one person at a time, because the tool is there and the work is due.

So the useful question isn't whether. It's what they paste in.

Today, send your team one line: what must never go into ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini. Client names, staff records and bank details is a good start. Then ask who's already using it, and for what.

This is not legal advice.

Source: DSIT, Cyber security skills in the UK labour market 2026, 29 September 2026. 230 cyber security businesses, fieldwork August to October 2025. https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cyber-security-skills-in-the-uk-labour-market-2026
```

```json
{"template":"stat","number":"7 in 10","meaning":"UK cyber security firms say their staff use AI day to day. Up from 53% a year earlier.","action":"Assume your team uses it too. Send one line today: what never gets pasted in.","source":"DSIT, 29 September 2026"}
```

**Honest verdict.** This is the best version of a borrowed-sector number, and it is still borrowed. It passes the relevance test only through the "the cautious go first" sentence, which is our read rather than the source's. It should run in an evergreen do-this slot, not as a wide post. If the slot needs a number about the reader's own firms, the same report has one: "This year 808,000, or 57%, of UK businesses reported having a basic technical skills gap", up from 49%, with the widest gap in "detecting and removing malware". That is about all UK businesses, which makes it the stronger stat post, but it is about cyber skills, not AI, so it belongs to a different week.

## 6. Eight ways this post fails, and the fix

| # | Failure | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Somebody else's number | "13% of UK construction firms", "70% of cyber firms" put in front of a dental practice | Run the relevance test. Use the all-business figure or the size band nearest 20 staff. If the reader's sector is not in the source, find the figure that includes them |
| 2 | The unit swap | "70% of firms say staff use AI" written as "7 in 10 of your staff" | Keep the unit. Firms translate per firm, people per person, money per pound. Self-check line 2 |
| 3 | The roundup of surveys | Four surveys, four numbers (20%, 35%, 47%, 48%), and the reader has to referee them | One number leads. At most two supporting figures, from the same document. A comparison of methods is a different post, if it is a post at all |
| 4 | The fact with no job | Ends "worth sitting with" or on our insights link. The card has a number and nothing to do | Every post and every card carries one action doable this week in under 30 minutes. If you cannot write one, the number is not for this page |
| 5 | The vague action | "Think about your AI policy", "review your adoption" | Name the person, the thing and the time: "Send your team three lines today: which tool, what never goes in, who to ask" |
| 6 | The second-hand or stale figure | A number seen in a newsletter, or an old edition presented as current | Open the primary document in this run, read the date on it, quote the sentence. Older than 14 days goes in an evergreen slot with the date in the first two lines |
| 7 | The overclaim | "Most firms", "AI is replacing admin staff", rounding 24% to "a quarter" and then to "hardly anyone" | Say what the source says, in its population and period. Cautious rounding only. Mark interpretation "our read". Say when the sample is not published |
| 8 | The teasing card | Eyebrow ("THE SECTOR THAT WORRIES MOST"), footer kicker, four type sizes, an empty band under the source | Stat schema only: number, meaning, action, source. No eyebrow, no kicker. The card must be usable with no caption |
