# 01. The cheat sheet: "do this Monday"

Written 11 October 2026. Replaces the six-jobs grid the owner rejected. This file holds the aim,
the evidence, the production prompt a batch run uses verbatim, two worked examples, the
six-jobs rewrite and the fail list. Where it disagrees with `docs/25`, `docs/29` and the card
law in the shared brief win; where it disagrees with `docs/01`, `docs/01` wins.

---

## 1. Aim

**The reader moment.** A practice manager, an estate agent or the owner of a builders'
merchant is scrolling on a phone between jobs. They have ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini somewhere,
used it twice, and were not sure what to ask. They see one image that names a job they did
this week and shows the exact words to type for it. They do not need to read the caption to
use it. They screenshot it, save it, or send it to the person in the office who will actually
do the job.

**The metric: saves and sends.** Not reactions, not comments.

- A cheat sheet asks for nothing to be argued with, so comments are the wrong scoreboard. The
  action a useful sheet earns is "keep this" or "you should see this".
- Our reader forwards privately more than they repost publicly. An owner sends a sheet to their
  office manager on WhatsApp or in a LinkedIn message; they rarely repost it to their own
  network. That is our read, not a measured fact.
- LinkedIn now counts both. It added saves and "sends on LinkedIn" to post analytics in
  September 2025 ([Social Media Today, LinkedIn adds save and send data to content insights,
  10 September 2025](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-adds-save-and-send-data-to-content-insights/759828/)).
  We have not confirmed these appear for company pages or through Buffer's metrics; check on
  LinkedIn itself before the first weekly pass relies on them.
- Secondary: reposts. Every high-repost post in our winners set is a list or a library
  (`references/winners/index.md`, "What the multiples say", point 1).

**Why this style earns the save when the six-jobs grid did not.** The grid gave a teaser per
cell ("THE POLICY NOBODY READS") and a summary of an instruction ("Paste it in. Ask for the same
rules at reading age twelve"). Nobody saves a summary. They save the thing they will copy next
week. Every value on this card is that thing: the words to type, or the check to run.

## 2. What works

### Our own evidence

- **Our best-rated AIFC post is a prompt library.** The 25-prompts card on Viren's profile, 23
  September 2026: 4.3% engagement on 674 impressions, the highest rate in the profile's history
  at the time (`docs/21`, section 2). One post, so a hypothesis, not a law. It points the same
  way as everything below.
- **Grids beat news on rate on our page.** Week to 21 September: two grids at 15.1% mean
  engagement against six news cards at 5.84%, on about half the impressions (`docs/21`). Two
  posts; one person moves the number.
- **The winners are libraries and procedures.** `references/winners/hassid-contact.jpg` and
  `hills-contact.jpg`, opened for this doc. Hassid's top non-video posts carry 77 to 306 reposts
  each; Hills' 119 to 218 (`references/winners/img/hassid.json`, `hills.json`). Both formats
  are a list of usable things, each with a name and what it does.
- **The cleanest card in LinkedIn's own curated top content is a numbered list with a bold
  lead-in.** `references/top-content/img/tc-62.jpg`, Josh Payne, 39,054 followers: cream ground,
  one typeface, a two-line title in a single highlight band, ten items, each a bold three-word
  lead then one or two plain sentences, text filling the canvas edge to edge. Our own scoring
  gave it the highest combined score of the hundred (`references/top-content/index.md`). It is
  the closest existing picture of the card law.

### Outside evidence, opened 11 October 2026

- **Portrait single images beat square and landscape.** Across 368,489 single-image posts,
  median engagement 2.83% for portrait, 2.54% square, 2.14% landscape; the page recommends
  1080x1350. Date range for that sub-sample is not stated. Captions of 800 to 900 characters
  showed 1.04x reach, and over 1,000 "don't add more reach". Documents account for 12.92% of
  all saved posts, about 2.6x their share of content; no save figure is given for images.
  Personal profiles, 3M+ posts, March 2025 to February 2026
  ([AuthoredUp, Best performing content on LinkedIn, updated 2 September 2026](https://authoredup.com/blog/best-performing-content-on-linkedin)).
- **Saves cluster on lists, guides, infographics and frameworks.** Van der Blom's Algorithm
  Insights Report 2025, 1.8M+ posts, as summarised by Mercer-MacKay; no per-metric sample and
  no save figure published
  ([Mercer-MacKay, A leader's guide to the LinkedIn algorithm, 2 June 2025](https://mercermackay.com/thinking/blog/a-leaders-guide-to-the-linkedin-algorithm-what-the-data-says/)).
  Secondary source; we could not open the report itself.
- **Reach for active creators is down about 60% over two years**, from van der Blom's 2026
  study of 1.3 million posts from 50,000 creators, said on the record. He did not give figures
  for saves or sends in that interview
  ([Creator Science podcast, episode 307, 2 June 2026](https://podcast.creatorscience.com/richard-van-der-blom-2/)).
  The relevance: the feed rewards the post people keep, not the post people glance at.

**Not usable.** Several 2026 blogs claim saves are now "the top signal" or that infographics get
"3x reach" (for example getdewey.co, 31 March 2026). We opened them: none cites a LinkedIn
statement, a sample or a method. Do not quote them.

### Accounts that do this well, and exactly what they do

1. **Ruben Hassid, "Claude Secret Codes" and "26 (free) Claude Skills"** (2,161 reactions, 269
   reposts; 1,654 reactions, 148 reposts). Hook: a flat claim under 50 characters ("Almost
   nobody knows Claude has secret codes."). Layout: a white sheet, a serif title, then a
   three-column table: name, when to use it, the exact command. Density: 26 rows, small type.
   Image versus caption: the image is the reference, the caption (1,073 to 1,813 characters)
   explains how to install and use it. His curated top post (`top-content/img/tc-13.jpg`)
   puts the whole prompt in the caption too, so it can be copied.
   **Take:** the "when to use it" column, the exact command, the copyable caption.
   **Refuse:** 26 rows in type nobody can read at half size on a phone.
2. **Charlie Hills, "22 Skills to Install in Claude"** (1,563 reactions, 215 reposts). Hook:
   "You installed Claude Code and stopped there." Layout: a dark code-editor frame, items grouped
   under four coloured headers (Build, Design, Research, Marketing), each line a name plus a
   one-line what-it-does. Caption 2,540 characters.
   **Take:** grouping by the reader's job, a number in the title. **Refuse:** dark,
   developer-styled density, four accent colours, tiny type. Our reader is not a developer and
   would feel the card was not for them.
3. **Josh Payne, "10 red flags I look for as an angel investor"** (`tc-62.jpg`, 39,054
   followers, recruitment). Hook: the title is the hook. Layout: one font, two sizes, bold
   lead-in per item, no boxes, no gaps, no illustration. Caption 1,851 characters, and it
   repeats the ten items as text.
   **Take:** all of it. This is our layout. **Refuse:** nothing structural; his items are
   opinions, ours must be instructions.
4. **Chris Donnelly, "10 AI Skills You Need To Know In 2026"** (1,784 reactions, 222 reposts,
   `donnelly-contact.jpg`). Layout: a ten-panel grid, each panel "what it is / when to use it /
   tools" with tool logos.
   **Take:** naming the tools so the reader knows it works in what they have. **Refuse:** at
   least four type sizes, panels with borders, text too small to read in feed, and the
   comment-for-the-resource close (`docs/01`, section 6).
5. **The SME-focused gap.** Heather Murray ("AI for Non-Techies", UK, our closest competitor)
   is the obvious SME-facing account, and her top ten in our read contains no cheat sheet at all
   (`references/creators/heather-murray.md`). We found no account doing prompt cheat sheets for
   UK small businesses by sector that we could verify. Every strong cheat-sheet account writes
   for AI enthusiasts or founders. A sheet titled for a dental practice or a builders' merchant
   is a slot nobody holds.

### What we take, what we refuse

| Take | Refuse |
|---|---|
| The exact words on the card, in quotation marks | Teasers and summaries of instructions |
| Bold lead-in label, value runs on (Payne) | Boxed cells with gaps between them (our six-jobs grid) |
| A job the reader did this week, named in their words | Tools, features or settings as the subject |
| One typeface, two sizes, text filling the canvas | Five type sizes, eyebrows, slogans, footer taglines |
| 3 to 6 items | 22, 26 or 42 items in unreadable type |
| The copyable version in the caption | "Comment PROMPTS and we'll send it" |
| A sector in the title, sometimes | "Founders", "creators", "power users" |

## 3. The production prompt

Paste everything inside the fence into a batch run, then add the inputs at the end.

```text
You are writing one LinkedIn post for the AI for Companies (AIFC) company page, in the
"cheat sheet" style: a single portrait image (1080x1350) that a busy UK business owner
screenshots, saves or forwards because it contains the actual usable thing, plus a caption.

READ FIRST, in the repo at /home/claude/aifc-social: docs/01-voice.md, docs/29-cards-that-get-read.md,
docs/prompts/01-cheatsheet.md sections 4 to 6 (worked examples and the fail list). If anything
below conflicts with docs/01, docs/01 wins.

INPUTS (the run supplies these; if one is missing, choose it and say what you chose)
- SECTOR: one UK SME sector, or "general" (dental practice, estate agent, accountant, builders'
  merchant, recruitment agency, cafe, solicitor, charity, trades, retail, care provider...).
- MOMENT: the job or the time of the week (e.g. "Monday inbox", "end of month", "a complaint
  comes in", "hiring", "a supplier quote arrives"). Optional; if empty, pick one that the
  sector does every week.
- DATE: the publish date, written as 20 July 2026.
- SOURCES: any document the post must cite. Usually none: a cheat sheet of words to type needs
  no statistic. If you use a figure, you must have opened its source in this run.
- RECENT TITLES: titles of the last ten posts, so you do not repeat a title formula twice in a row.

AUDIENCE
Owners and managers of UK small and medium businesses, about 5 to 250 staff. They have heard of
ChatGPT, may have tried it once or twice, may already pay for Microsoft 365 (Copilot) or Google
Workspace (Gemini). Not technical, slightly intimidated. They must never feel stupid, lectured
or sold to. What they want: what do I type, on what, and what do I get back, today. Write at
reading age about 12. Contractions are good. Name tools plainly: "ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini,
whichever you have".
Never use: agentic, LLM, tenant, roadmap, GA, Purview, use case, workflow automation, leverage,
transformation, prompt engineering, delve, utilise, robust, seamless, unlock, harness, elevate,
supercharge, game changer, landscape, realm, crucial, vital, pivotal, honestly, genuinely,
straightforward, "in today's", "let's dive in", "here's the thing", "most people", "read that
again", "it's not X, it's Y".

STEP 1. PICK THE MOMENT
One moment per sheet. It must be a job the reader recognises from their own last week, in their
words ("a cross review on Google", not "reputation management"). If the sector is set, every
item belongs to that sector's week. If you cannot name the moment in five words, it is too vague.

STEP 2. LIST TEN CANDIDATE ITEMS, KEEP THE BEST 3 TO 6
Each candidate is: the situation (what lands on their desk) + the exact words to type, or a
check to run. Keep an item only if it passes all six tests:
  a. Desk test: it uses something they already have (an email, a PDF quote, a rota, a review,
     meeting notes, a price list). No new software, no sign-up, no setup.
  b. Ten-minute test: done start to finish in under ten minutes, first time.
  c. Three-tool test: works the same in ChatGPT, Copilot and Gemini on a basic plan.
  d. Checkable: the reader can tell in one read whether the answer is right.
  e. Safe: it does not ask them to paste customer, patient or staff personal details. If the
     job needs personal material, one item must be the check that removes it first, and the
     caption must end "This is not legal advice."
  f. Not obvious: an owner who has used ChatGPT twice would not have thought of asking this
     exact way. "Write an email" fails. "Write it three ways: short, warm, firm" passes.
Prefer a mix: mostly words-to-type items, and at most one check item ("Before you send it...").
Order them in the order the week happens, or easiest first.

STEP 3. WRITE EACH ITEM
- label: the situation in 2 to 5 words, in the reader's words. It says what is on their desk,
  not a clever name. Good: "A supplier quote arrives". Bad: "The quote nobody reads".
  Where something must be pasted or attached, say so in the label: "Meeting notes (paste them)".
- value: THE THING ITSELF. Either
    (i) the exact words to type, inside double quotation marks, with [square brackets] for the
        part they fill in. Written so it works if typed exactly as shown. Or
    (ii) a check, as an instruction they can carry out in under a minute.
  Value rules:
    - Never a teaser, never a summary of an instruction ("Ask it for owners and dates" FAILS;
      "List every action as who, what, by when. Write 'no owner' where nobody's named." PASSES).
    - Up to 22 words. With 6 items, up to about 16 words each.
    - Each words-to-type value includes one detail that makes the answer usable: a word limit,
      a format (table, list, three versions), or a rule ("keep every price exactly as written",
      "mark anything not stated").
    - Plain words a 12-year-old can read. No AI jargon, no role-play openers ("Act as a world
      class...").
    - UK spelling. No em dashes, no double hyphens, no exclamation marks, no emoji.
- Fit budget: all labels and values together between 450 and 750 characters. Under 450 leaves
  empty bands on the card; over 750 forces type below 34px. Count it.

STEP 4. WRITE THE TITLE
Up to 12 words. It names the moment and promises the usable thing. Statement, not question.
No eyebrow above it, no subtitle under it. Use one of these formulas, and not the same one as
the previous cheat sheet in RECENT TITLES:
  1. What to type when [moment]
     "What to type when a customer complaint lands"
  2. [N] [sector] jobs, and the exact words to type
     "Five front-desk jobs, and the exact words to type"
  3. [Sector role]'s cheat sheet: [moment]
     "Practice manager's cheat sheet: the front desk week"
  4. Copy these into ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini [time]
     "Copy these into ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini before Monday"
  5. Before you [send / sign / post] anything AI wrote, check these [N]
     "Before you send anything AI wrote, check these four things"
  6. [N] things to type before the [time/rush]
     "Five things to type before the morning rush"
  7. Stuck on [task]? Type this. (beforeafter only, the one permitted question)
     "Stuck on a job advert? Type this instead."
  8. The words that get [result] from [document]
     "The words that get a straight answer from a supplier quote"
Numbers as numerals or words, consistently within the post. No "hacks", "secrets", "tricks",
"insane", "game-changing".

STEP 5. THE CARD JSON
Default:
{"template":"cheatsheet","title":"","items":[{"label":"","value":""}],"source":""}
- 3 to 6 items.
- source: if the post cites a document, "Publisher, title, date". If not, use
  "Works in ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini" so the reader knows it works in what they have.
- Nothing else on the card. No logo row, no tagline, no "Try this week", no URL.
Use beforeafter instead only when the whole value is one swap: what people type versus what
works.
{"template":"beforeafter","title":"","before":{"label":"What most people type","text":""},
 "after":{"label":"Type this instead","text":""}}
The after text is the full words to type, under 45 words. The before text is a real, common
weak prompt, under 12 words.

CARD LAW (the renderer enforces layout; you enforce content)
1. The card alone gives something usable. If a reader only saw the image, could they do the job
   now? If not, rewrite.
2. Title and value. Nothing else competes.
3. One typeface, two content sizes, one small footer size.
4. Content fills 1080x1350 with even rhythm. Your character budget is what makes that possible.
5. Body text never below about 34px at 1080 wide.
6. Brand green as the single accent. One small AIforCompanies wordmark on the footer at most.
A save line on the card is allowed only in docs/29's exact words ("Save for Friday", "Save for
your next meeting", "Save for your next quote", "Share with a friend"), at most one, only if the
template has a slot. Never put it in the caption.

STEP 6. THE CAPTION
Length: 1,100 to 1,700 characters including spaces. Count it.
Structure:
  1. Hook, first line, under 70 characters, and inside 140 for certain. A flat statement that
     names the moment or the sector. Not a question. Not "Most people".
     e.g. "Five front-desk jobs at a dental practice, and the words to type for each."
  2. Permission, one or two sentences. Who it is for and that they already have the tool.
     e.g. "If you've opened ChatGPT twice and not known what to ask, start with these. They
     work in ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever you already have."
  3. The copyable list. Numbered, one per item: the label, what to paste or attach if
     anything, then the exact words in quotation marks, identical to the card. This is what
     lets a phone reader copy and paste. Nothing else in the item, or at most one short clause
     on what good looks like.
  4. One paragraph, two or three sentences, on the one thing to watch: usually "check every
     name, price and date against the original before you send". Specific to this sector.
  5. Close, one of:
     - Resource line: "There are 25 more, sorted by job, at aiforcompanies.co.uk/prompts."
       (at most one cheat sheet in two carries a link)
     - Nothing: end on the watch-out paragraph.
     Never "save this", "follow for more", "comment X", "agree?", "which will you try first?".
  6. If any item touches UK GDPR (personal data), employment (hiring, staff, rotas, contracts
     of employment) or contracts: last line exactly "This is not legal advice."
Voice: "we" is AI for Companies, "you" is the reader, never "I". UK English. Dates as 20 July
2026. No em dashes, no double hyphens, no exclamation marks, no emoji, no hashtags, no italics,
no arrows as bullets. Vary sentence length; do not stack one-sentence paragraphs. No invented
clients, results, quotes or testimonials; AIFC has no delivery history. Illustrative framing
("say you run a dental practice") is fine. Every statistic carries its source and date and you
must have opened the source in this run; if not, cut the statistic.

STEP 7. SELF-CHECK. Answer each yes or no in your notes. Any "no" means rewrite before output.
 [ ] Could someone use the card without reading the caption?
 [ ] Is every value the words to type or a check, never a teaser or summary?
 [ ] Does every words-to-type value contain a usability detail (limit, format or rule)?
 [ ] Are there 3 to 6 items, labels 2 to 5 words, values 22 words or fewer?
 [ ] Is the label+value total between 450 and 750 characters?
 [ ] Is the title 12 words or fewer, a statement, naming the moment?
 [ ] Would an owner who has used ChatGPT twice understand every word on the card?
 [ ] Does every item pass the desk, ten-minute and three-tool tests?
 [ ] Does any item ask for personal data to be pasted? If yes, is the removal check an item
     and does the caption end "This is not legal advice."?
 [ ] Caption 1,100 to 1,700 characters; hook under 70; copyable words identical to the card?
 [ ] No banned word, no em dash, no double hyphen, no exclamation mark, no emoji, no hashtag,
     no "I", no engagement bait?
 [ ] No invented client, result or quote; any statistic sourced, dated and opened this run?
 [ ] Read the title and first caption line aloud. Would an office manager say them to a
     colleague? If they sound like a caption about the card, rewrite.

OUTPUT, in this order:
1. CAPTION: the full text.
2. CARD: the JSON, valid, on its own.
3. NOTES: moment chosen; title formula number; character counts (caption, label+value total);
   legal line yes/no and why; sources opened with URLs, or "none"; the self-check answers.
```

## 4. Two worked examples

Produced by running the prompt above. Character counts measured, not estimated.

### Example A. Dental practice

**Inputs.** SECTOR: dental practice. MOMENT: the front-desk week. DATE: 20 October 2026.
SOURCES: none. Title formula 3.

**Caption** (1,359 characters)

```text
Five front-desk jobs at a dental practice, and the words to type for each.

If you've opened ChatGPT once or twice and not known what to ask, start here. These work in ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever the practice already has.

1. A cross review online. Paste the review, then type: "Write a calm public reply to this review. Thank them, don't mention any treatment, offer a call. Under 60 words."

2. A missed appointment. Type: "Write a friendly note to a patient who missed their appointment. Mention our fee policy once. Offer two new times."

3. A new fee list. Paste the list, then type: "Turn this into a one-page sheet patients can read in a minute. Keep every price exactly as written."

4. A rota clash. Paste the rota, then type: "List every day in this rota with fewer than two nurses on. Dates only."

5. Before you paste anything. Take out names, dates of birth and anything about treatment. Write "the patient" instead. The reply is just as good without them.

The one to watch is the review reply. A public reply that confirms someone is a patient, or mentions their treatment, is the thing that turns a bad review into a complaint. The prompt says "don't mention any treatment" for that reason, so check it did as it was told before you post.

There are 25 more, sorted by job, at aiforcompanies.co.uk/prompts.

This is not legal advice.
```

### Example B. Builders' merchant

**Inputs.** SECTOR: builders' merchant. MOMENT: before the morning rush. DATE: 22 October 2026.
SOURCES: none. Title formula 6.

**Caption** (1,374 characters)

```text
Five jobs at a builders' merchant, and what to type before the morning rush.

They work in ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever you already pay for. If you've only used it to write a birthday message, these are a better place to start.

1. A supplier quote arrives. Attach the PDF, then type: "Put every price, delivery charge and lead time from this quote in a table. Mark anything not stated."

2. A customer chasing a late order. Type: "Write a short reply to a customer whose order is [days] late because [reason]. Give the new date. No excuses."

3. A delivery that looks short. Paste the delivery note and your order, then type: "List anything missing, short or different between this delivery note and our order, with the line number."

4. A product page with no description. Type: "Write 50 words on [product] for tradespeople: size, what it's for, what it fits. No sales language."

5. Before anything goes out. Check every price, quantity and date against the original document, not from memory.

The quote table is the one that saves the most time. "Mark anything not stated" is the part doing the work: the gaps it marks, a missing delivery charge or no lead time, are the questions to ring the supplier about before you order.

Number 5 matters more here than anywhere. An assistant can copy a figure wrong, and on a trade quote one wrong number is real money.
```

## 5. Before and after: the six-jobs grid

**Before** (`bank/posts/2026-09-23-six-jobs-grid.md`, `cards/2026-09-23-six-jobs-grid.png`).
Card: "SIX JOBS TO HAND YOUR ASSISTANT THIS WEEK" over a 2x3 grid of boxed cells, each a capitalised
teaser ("THE POLICY NOBODY READS") and a summary ("Paste it in. Ask for the same rules at reading
age twelve, on one page."). Five type sizes, three empty bands between rows, wordmark plus "TRY
THIS WEEK" footer. Caption: good observations, but the reader learns what to ask for, never the
words, and the close ("Pick one. Not six.") tells them to do less with a card that already gave
them too little.

What changes: each teaser becomes a plain label; each summary becomes the exact words; the
card becomes one list that fills the canvas; the caption carries the copyable words; the job
advert gets the legal line it was missing.

**After: caption** (1,367 characters)

```text
Six jobs you'd do this week anyway, and the exact words to type for each.

They work in ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever you have. None needs anything you don't already have on your desk.

1. A policy nobody reads. Paste it in: "Rewrite this for staff reading on a phone. One page, plain words, keep every rule."

2. Notes from a meeting. Paste them: "List every action as who, what, by when. Write 'no owner' where nobody's named."

3. An email you're putting off. Type: "I need to tell [who] that [what]. Write it three ways: short, warm, firm."

4. Two lists that should match. Paste both: "Show what's in the first list but not the second, then the other way round."

5. Before a hard call. Type: "I'm calling [who] about [what]. What will they push back on? One-line answer to each."

6. A job advert. Type: "Write an advert for [role]. We want someone who [three things]. Under 200 words, no jargon."

What they have in common: you're not asking it to know anything. You're asking it to do something to material you already have, which is where it's most reliable and where most of the time goes.

Two checks before you use the answers. Read the rewritten policy against the original, because "keep every rule" is an instruction it can still miss. And read the advert for anything that hints at age or gender before it goes live.

This is not legal advice.
```

## 6. Fail list: eight ways this style goes boring or corporate

| # | How it fails | One-line fix |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The teaser label.** "The policy nobody reads", "The quiet time thief". Clever names that make the reader work. | Name what is on their desk in their words: "A policy nobody reads (paste it)". |
| 2 | **The summarised instruction.** "Ask it for owners and dates." The reader still has to write the prompt. | Put the exact words in quotation marks, ready to type. |
| 3 | **The tool-first title.** "5 Copilot features for productivity." Our reader does not think in features. | Title the moment: "What to type when a supplier quote arrives". |
| 4 | **The generic office.** Items that could belong to any company of any size ("summarise a report"). | Pick a sector or a moment and make every item belong to it. |
| 5 | **The obvious prompt.** "Write an email to a customer." They already tried that and got mush. | Add the one detail that makes it usable: three versions, a word limit, "mark anything not stated". |
| 6 | **Too many items, too small.** Eight or more items, or 25-word values, push type under 34px. | 3 to 6 items, 450 to 750 characters in total; cut the weakest item, not the font size. |
| 7 | **The corporate frame.** "Drive efficiency across your operations", "empower your team". Reads like a vendor brochure. | Write it as an office manager would say it to a colleague, then check it against the banned list. |
| 8 | **The caption that explains the card instead of giving the copy.** "These prompts will transform your week." Phone readers cannot copy text from an image. | Caption carries the numbered, copyable words, identical to the card, then one watch-out. |
