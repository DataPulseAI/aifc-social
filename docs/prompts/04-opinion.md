# 04. The opinion post

Written 11 October 2026. Governs the position band in `docs/22` section 2 (two in ten posts).
Where this disagrees with `docs/22` section 3 on shape, this wins for the small-business reader.
`docs/01` (voice) and the shared card law still win over this file.

---

## 1. Aim, reader moment, metric

**Aim.** Say out loud the sensible thing a small business owner half-thinks about AI and
nobody else is saying, so they feel seen, want to argue or agree in the comments, and
start to trust that AI for Companies talks sense. Credibility, not leads. The post never
mentions training, the offer or us.

**The reader moment.** A practice manager, an estate agent, a builders' merchant owner,
scrolling on a phone at 7.40am before the doors open or in the van between jobs. They have
tried ChatGPT once or twice. They have read that they are behind. They see one sentence that
makes AI smaller, cheaper and more doable than everyone else has made it sound, and that
sentence is about their week, not a corporate's.

**Metric, in order.**

1. **Comments with a sentence in them**, not one-word reactions.
2. **Threaded replies**: replies inside those comments, ours and other readers'. That is the
   sign people are arguing with each other, which is what we want.
3. **Reposts**, ideally with a line of their own on top ("this is exactly what I keep saying").

Reactions and impressions are recorded but are not the target.

**Why these three.** The page has a tiny following, so it travels only when other people
carry it. `docs/23` section 1 found that what gets talked about is "a person, a position or a
correction" and that nobody comments on a changelog; the position is the one of the three a
company page can always produce. Comments are worth most early: `references/claims.md` records
the sourced neighbour of the first-hour claim (three or more meaningful comments in the first
hour, van der Blom via Blueberry Media). A comment only becomes a thread if someone answers
it, so the post is not finished when it publishes: see the operating note at the end of
section 3.

## 2. What works

### Our own references

- **`docs/22` section 3** sets the shape: claim in the first line that someone could disagree
  with, two to four paragraphs of argument, one concrete "what we'd do instead" paragraph,
  then stop. No link, no question. We keep all of that. What changes is the reader: the two
  rejected posts argued about business cases and pilots, which a 20-person firm does not have.
- **`docs/23` section 1 and `references/winners/index.md`**: permission outperforms
  instruction. Hassid "if you use AI and feel like you're falling behind: stop doom-scrolling
  AI news, pick ONE tool and go deep" (2.5x his median), "You can't keep up with AI. Me
  neither" (1.9x), Donnelly's "The average millionaire is 61" (5.8x), Heather Murray admitting
  she has never used Claude Code (1.5x). The opinions that land with non-experts make the
  problem smaller and give permission. That is our stance in `docs/01` section 2, "the calm
  thing in a loud room".
- **Isenberg** (`references/creators/chia-and-isenberg.md`): his top post is text only, 565
  characters, a numbered opinion list, no image, no link, no question. Short is not punished
  (`references/winners/index.md`, finding 6).
- **Alex Banks** (`references/creators/alex-banks.md`): "Forced AI features are quietly
  creating AI sceptics" (394) is an opinion about the buyer's own experience, and his 7.0x
  satire post names the reader's industry habit. Opinions about the reader's week beat
  opinions about the market.
- **`references/authoredup/index.md`**: contrarian openers sit second only to story (2.31%
  median engagement against 2.27% for a statement and 2.16% for a question, 309,614 posts,
  December 2025 to May 2026); openers of 40 characters or fewer earn the most; a closing
  question moves the median by 0.07 of a point. Its rule 10: "Contrarian only where you
  actually disagree. Forced contrarianism reads as bait." Its worked examples give us the
  two-line contradiction (Jenny Anderson, "Women don't like sales. No. You're wrong.") and the
  flat "X is NOT Y" claim (Erin Balsa).
- **`references/top-content/index.md`**: 72 of LinkedIn's 100 curated posts open with a flat
  statement; 36 of 100 carry no image.
- **Our own card failure.** `cards/2026-10-08-headcount-business-case.png` is a statement card
  with an "OUR POSITION" eyebrow, a URL, a sentence in the middle third and two empty bands
  above and below. It breaks card law rules 1, 2 and 4: there is nothing to use, and half the
  canvas is empty.

**What that means for the format.** Text wins comments on people's profiles. On a company
page, text is the weakest format measured. So:

- **On the page: the statement card**, with the caption carrying the argument. The card earns
  the stop and, through its sub line, gives the reader the usable action even if they never
  tap "see more".
- **On Viren's profile: text only**, same words in the first person allowed by `docs/23`
  section 5, 1,000 to 1,400 characters. That is where text earns its comments.

Nobody has published a measured figure for opinion posts as a category. The case for the band
rests on our creator read and on the comment data above, and we say so.

### Creators who land sharp, plain opinions with non-experts

1. **Greg Isenberg** (our read, `references/creators/chia-and-isenberg.md`). First line: the
   whole claim, flat ("The only businesses left to build"). Length: 565 characters. Rhythm: a
   numbered list of short claims, each one line. Ending: the last item, then nothing. No
   image, no link, no question. Take: the claim is the hook and the post can be short.
2. **Justin Welsh** (`references/creators/justin-welsh.md`). First line: a flat declarative that
   states the whole idea ("To build a business on LinkedIn, you need better content"). Length:
   around 1,000 characters. Rhythm: claim, reasoning, the complete lesson; nothing depends on a
   click. Ending: the lesson's last line. Card: one sentence on a plain ground with one phrase
   highlighted. Take: the reader who never clicks still leaves with the lesson; one sentence on
   a plain card.
3. **Daniel Priestley** (UK, writes to small business owners). Seen in excerpts at
   [ViralBrain, Daniel Priestley profile, undated, 14 posts analysed](https://www.viralbrain.ai/heroes/daniel-priestley-94ksslol):
   "9 out of 10 businesses fail." then "Nonsense." on its own line, then a UK figure and an
   argument; "The biggest podcasts in the world don't book people they've never heard of."
   First line: a belief the reader holds or a fact that frames it. Rhythm: mostly one-line
   paragraphs. Ending: not visible in the excerpts. Take: the two-line contradiction, aimed at
   a belief owners actually hold. Refuse: the one-line-per-paragraph stack (`docs/01` section 4).
4. **Ruben Hassid** (`references/creators/ruben-hassid.md`). First line: the reader's feeling,
   named ("You can't keep up with AI. Me neither."). Rhythm: two or three short sentences of
   relief, then the smaller plan ("pick one AI, pick one task you already do every week, define
   what good looks like"). Ending: the plan. Take: permission plus a smaller step is the shape
   that travels. Refuse: "Me neither" in our mouth, because we are a company and we do keep up.
5. **Heather Murray** (`references/creators/heather-murray.md`), our nearest competitor, same
   buyer. First line: an admission or a flat statement in a warm British voice. Length: long,
   about 1,650 characters. Ending: often a PS or a question, which we do not copy. Take: you do
   not have to know everything is a position this buyer trusts, and the voice is warm, not
   clever.

### What we take

- The claim in the first line, under about 80 characters, complete on its own.
- The take that makes AI smaller, cheaper or sooner than the reader feared.
- The belief in the reader's own words, then the answer.
- The usable step, in the post and on the card.
- Ending on the step or a hard sentence, then stopping.

### What we refuse

- **Rage bait.** Attacking a group of people (young staff, older staff, a profession, people
  who don't use AI), or picking a fight for the sake of the comments. We aim at a habit, a
  belief or the hype, never at the reader or a type of person.
- **Fake vulnerability.** "We got this wrong for years." The company has no history to confess.
- **"Unpopular opinion:", "Hot take:", "Controversial, but"**, and any label that announces the
  opinion instead of stating it. If it is genuinely unpopular the reader will notice.
- **"Most people are doing X wrong"** and every "Most people" opener (`docs/01`).
- **The question close, "Agree?", "Thoughts?"**, and any pitch.

## 3. The production prompt

```text
You are writing one opinion post for the AI for Companies LinkedIn page. AI for Companies is a
UK company that trains staff at small and medium businesses to use the AI tools they already
have. The post never mentions training, our offer, our website or us.

INPUTS
- date: the publish date, written as 20 July 2026.
- channel: "page" (statement card plus caption) or "profile" (text only, first person allowed).
- seed (optional): a take, a reader comment, or a fear to work from.
- recent: the first lines of the last ten opinion posts. Do not repeat a take or a first-line
  formula used in the last four.
- sector (optional): one sector to anchor the examples (dental practice, estate agent,
  accountant, builders' merchant, recruitment agency, cafe, solicitor, charity, trades).

THE READER
An owner or manager of a UK business with 5 to 250 staff. They have heard of ChatGPT, may have
tried it once or twice, may pay for Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace. Not technical, a bit
intimidated, busy, and tired of being told they are behind. They must never feel stupid,
lectured or sold to. Write at about reading age 12. Contractions are good.

STEP 1. FIND A TAKE THEY GENUINELY HOLD OR FEAR
Start from one of these, not from a news story or a report:
- A fear: we're behind; we'll waste money; staff will paste in something they shouldn't; staff
  will think it's coming for their jobs; we'll look stupid; we'll pick the wrong tool; we
  haven't got time to learn it.
- A half-belief: we're too small for AI; we need a strategy first; we need to train everyone at
  once; the young ones will sort it; ban it to be safe; wait until it settles down; we need to
  buy something new; AI is for writing posts and emails.
- A loud claim from the hype they keep hearing: AI changes everything, you'll be left behind,
  you need agents, you need a consultant.
Then write the take that makes the problem smaller, cheaper or sooner: one job, one person, one
week, the tool they already have. That is our stance: the calm thing in a loud room.

Good takes look like these (do not reuse one used in the last four posts):
- You don't need an AI strategy, you need one person who tries it on Tuesday.
- The best first job for AI is the one nobody in your office wants.
- Don't buy another AI tool until someone's used the one you've got for a month.
- Your AI rules should fit on a sticky note.
- Don't hand AI to the youngest person in the office. Hand it to the one who knows the job best.
- Banning ChatGPT at work doesn't stop people using it. It stops them telling you.
- Give AI one job and four weeks, then decide.
- Ask AI to read your emails before you ask it to write them.
- Tell your team what AI is for before they decide for themselves.
- You're not behind. You just haven't picked the first job yet.

STEP 2. THE NOUN CHECK
Every thing the post mentions must be something a 20-person firm actually has: emails, quotes,
invoices, the rota, meeting notes, a price list, customer questions, reviews, site notes,
job adverts, a supplier, the team meeting, Monday morning.
Never: business case, pilot, project, rollout, roadmap, strategy document, stakeholders,
change management, governance, licences or seats, quarterly review, headcount, use case,
workflow, transformation, leverage, agentic, LLM, tenant, GA, Purview.
Name tools as "ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever you have".

STEP 3. ARGUABLE BUT NOT ALIENATING
Write down, privately, the best one-sentence counter-argument a sensible owner would make.
- If you cannot write one, the take is a platitude ("AI is a tool, not a replacement").
  Sharpen it until someone could disagree.
- Read the take as two readers. The sceptical owner should want to argue. The nervous owner
  should feel relieved. If the nervous owner would feel stupid, blamed or behind, rewrite.
- The target is a habit, a belief or the hype. Never the reader, never a group of people.
- We must be willing to defend the take politely in the comments for a week. If not, drop it.

STEP 4. THE FIRST LINE
Under about 80 characters where possible, never over 140. Complete on its own. A statement, not
a question. Choose one formula:
1. The permission. "You don't need X, you need Y."
   "You don't need an AI strategy, you need one person who tries it on Tuesday."
2. The belief, then the answer. Their words, then a short reply.
   "'We're too small for AI.' Small is the easiest size to start."
3. The refusal. "Don't buy X until Y."
   "Don't buy another AI tool until someone's used the one you've got for a month."
4. The swap. "Ask it to do Y before you ask it to do X."
   "Ask AI to read your emails before you ask it to write them."
5. The right person. "Don't hand it to X. Hand it to Y."
   "Don't hand AI to the youngest person in the office. Hand it to the one who knows the job best."
6. The size rule. "Your X should fit on Y."
   "Your AI rules should fit on a sticky note."
7. The plain rule. "If X, then Y."
   "If you wouldn't email it to a stranger, don't paste it into a free AI account."
8. The first-job claim. "The best first X is Y."
   "The best first job for AI is the one nobody in your office wants."
9. The time box. "Give it X, then decide."
   "Give AI one job and four weeks, then decide."
Never: a question, "Most people", "Unpopular opinion", "Hot take", "Here's the thing", our name.

STEP 5. THE BODY
Caption length: page 800 to 1,100 characters including the first line; profile text-only
1,000 to 1,400.
Shape:
- Paragraph 2: what actually happens in a small firm, told plainly, as an example clearly
  marked as an example ("say you run a dental practice") or as a general observation. Two or
  three sentences.
- Paragraph 3: why our take is right. One reason, not three. Where a number appears it carries
  its source and date and you have opened the source; otherwise use no number. Say "our read
  is" where it is our opinion.
- Paragraph 4: WHAT WE'D DO INSTEAD. Start "What we'd do:" or "Here's what we'd do." Concrete
  enough to do this week: who, which job, which day, with what tool, and what to look at
  afterwards. Name real jobs (the supplier chase, the meeting notes, the price list).
- Optional paragraph 5: what happens if it works and if it doesn't. Both outcomes are fine.
Rhythm: paragraphs of one to three sentences, lengths varied. Never more than two one-sentence
paragraphs in a row. No arrow bullets. At most one list of three in the whole post. No
"It's not X, it's Y" line. No "Amateurs do X, professionals do Y".

STEP 6. THE END
End on the step or on one hard sentence about what changes. Then stop.
Good: "After a month you'll know more than a strategy document would have told you."
Good: "People don't fight a tool they picked themselves."
Never: a question, "Agree?", "Thoughts?", a link, our name, training, "follow", "save",
"repost", "comment".
If the post gives any advice about hiring, firing, contracts, monitoring staff or personal
data, the last line is: This is not legal advice.

STEP 7. THE CARD (page only)
Output this JSON exactly:
{"template":"statement","text":"","sub":""}
- text: the take, one sentence, 14 words or fewer where possible, never over 16. It may differ
  slightly from the first line of the caption to fit.
- sub: REQUIRED for us even though the schema marks it optional. The usable step from
  paragraph 4, one line, 20 words or fewer, an instruction the reader could follow without the
  caption. A card with only the opinion teases and fails the card law.
- No eyebrow ("Our position"), no URL, no quote marks round the text, no source line unless the
  sub carries a number.

STEP 8. SELF-CHECK. Answer each line yes or no; any wrong answer means rewrite.
[ ] First line is a statement under 140 characters and makes sense alone.
[ ] I can write the counter-argument a sensible owner would make.
[ ] The nervous owner would feel relieved, not stupid or behind.
[ ] Every noun is something a 20-person firm has. No banned jargon.
[ ] There is a "what we'd do" paragraph a reader could act on this week.
[ ] The card sub line is a usable step on its own.
[ ] No question, link, pitch, mention of us or training, or engagement ask anywhere.
[ ] No em dashes, double hyphens, exclamation marks, emoji, hashtags or italics. UK spelling.
[ ] "We" is the company, "you" is the reader, no "I" (profile channel excepted).
[ ] No invented client, result, quote or "we've seen". Every number sourced, dated, opened.
[ ] No more than two one-sentence paragraphs in a row; no "It's not X, it's Y".
[ ] Legal line present if the post touches hiring, firing, contracts, staff monitoring or
    personal data.
[ ] Length in range for the channel.

OUTPUT
1. The caption, ready to paste.
2. The card JSON (page only).
3. One line: the counter-argument you expect, so whoever replies in the first hour is ready.
```

**Operating note, outside the prompt.** Threaded replies come from somebody answering. On the
morning an opinion post goes out, someone replies to every real comment within the first hour,
with a sentence that adds something (agrees and gives one more example, or disagrees politely
and says why). Viren reshares from his profile in the first hour with one sentence of his own
(`docs/22`, `docs/23` section 5). Without that, the metric in section 1 cannot move.

## 4. Two worked examples

### Example A: the permission

Caption (page, about 1,100 characters, the top of the range):

```text
You don't need an AI strategy. You need one person who tries it on Tuesday.

A strategy is what big companies write when they have a team to write it. In a firm of 15 or
50 it usually goes like this: someone says "we should look at AI", a meeting gets booked, and
three months later nobody has typed a word into it.

What moves things is smaller and a bit boring. One person, one job they do every week, and the
AI you already have. ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, whichever it is, even the free version.

Our read is that the person matters more than the tool. Pick someone who knows the job
inside out, not whoever is "good with computers". They'll know in seconds whether what comes
back is any use.

Here's what we'd do. On Monday, ask them to choose the weekly job they least like: the supplier
chase, the meeting notes, the price list that needs tidying. On Tuesday they try it with AI,
once. On Friday they tell you in two minutes what happened, good or bad.

If it helped, they do it again next week. If it didn't, they pick another job. After a month
you'll know more than a strategy document would have told you.
```

Card:

```json
{"template":"statement","text":"You don't need an AI strategy, you need one person who tries it on Tuesday.","sub":"Let them pick the weekly job they like least. Try it once. Hear how it went on Friday."}
```

Counter-argument to expect: "Without a plan you'll get people pasting customer data into free
tools." Reply: agree that one rule matters, and the rule fits on a sticky note (which is the
next opinion post).

### Example B: the first job

Caption (page, about 1,000 characters):

```text
The best first job for AI is the one nobody in your office wants.

The usual first try is something big: plan the business, write the website. What comes back is
bland, and it's easy to decide AI isn't for you. That's a fair reaction to an unfair test.

The jobs people put off are a much better test. Turning scribbled site notes into a tidy report.
The polite chase for an unpaid invoice. The same answer to the same customer question, reworded
for the tenth time. They're dull, they come round every week, and you can tell in ten seconds
whether the result is right.

That last part matters. If you can check it quickly, you'll trust it quickly. And the person who
hated the job becomes the one who wants to show everyone else.

What we'd do: at the next team catch-up, ask one question. "Which weekly job would you hand over
tomorrow if you could?" Write the answers down, pick the one that comes up most, and try it this
week with whatever you already have.

Start there. The big stuff can wait until someone trusts it.
```

Card:

```json
{"template":"statement","text":"The best first job for AI is the one nobody in your office wants.","sub":"Ask the team which weekly job they'd hand over tomorrow. Try that one first."}
```

Counter-argument to expect: "The dull jobs are the ones where mistakes cost money, like
invoices." Reply: that is why they are a good test, because you check every one and you will
see quickly whether to trust it.

## 5. Before and after

### 2026-10-08-headcount-business-case

**Before, first line:** "If your AI business case only works by cutting people, it is not an
AI business case. It is a redundancy plan wearing a new badge."

**Why it failed this reader.** A 20-person firm has no "business case", no "benefit case", no
"project" and no talk of "headcount" or "the wage bill". "Take 20% off forty jobs" assumes forty
jobs. The sharpest idea in it, that staff will quietly sink a tool they think is replacing them,
is real for a small firm and was buried in paragraph two. The card added an "OUR POSITION"
eyebrow and a URL, left half the canvas empty, and gave the reader nothing to do.

**After, caption:**

```text
If your team thinks AI is there to replace them, they'll quietly make sure it doesn't work.

Nobody will say it out loud. They'll try it once, get a so-so answer, and tell you it's not much
use. In a small firm, where everyone talks to everyone, that view spreads by Thursday.

You can't really blame them. Most of what they've read about AI is about jobs going. If you
bring it in without saying why, they'll fill the gap with the scariest version.

So say why, before anyone asks. In plenty of small businesses the honest answer is the boring
one: there's more work than people, and some of it is work nobody enjoys. Only say it if it's
true.

What we'd do: at the next team meeting, say in one sentence what AI is for here. Something like
"it's for the jobs we all put off, so we've got more time for customers." Then ask each person
to name one of those jobs, and let them choose what gets tried first.

People don't fight a tool they picked themselves.
```

**After, card:**

```json
{"template":"statement","text":"Tell your team what AI is for before they decide for themselves.","sub":"Say it in one sentence at the next team meeting. Then let them pick the first job."}
```

The post gives no advice on staffing decisions, so the legal line is not triggered. If an edit
adds anything about redundancy, contracts or monitoring staff, it ends with: This is not legal
advice.

### 2026-10-09-pilot-end-date

**Before, first line:** "Your AI pilot needs an end date, not a bigger budget."

**Why it failed this reader.** A small firm does not run a "pilot", has no "ten seats", no
"line item", no "quarterly review" and no "decision date". The idea underneath is right and
universal: AI in a small firm drifts rather than fails. The headline is also the banned
"not X, Y" shape.

**After, caption:**

```text
Give AI one job and four weeks. Then decide.

In a small firm, AI rarely gets switched off. It just stops being opened. Someone signs up, it's
handy for a fortnight, then a busy week arrives and everyone goes back to the old way. Nobody
decided anything. It drifted.

Our read is that drifting is the worst result, worse than trying it and saying no. A clear no
tells you something. Drift leaves you with a login nobody uses, a vague feeling you're behind,
and no idea whether it would have helped.

Here's what we'd do. Pick one job that comes round every week: the quote follow-ups, the meeting
notes, the rota email. Pick one person to do it with AI every time. Then open your diary, go
four weeks forward, and write one question in it: are we still using it for this, and is it
saving time?

When the day comes, take five minutes over it. If the answer is yes, add a second job. If it's
no, try a different job or stop. Both are fine.

What isn't fine is finding the login in six months and not remembering what it was for.
```

**After, card:**

```json
{"template":"statement","text":"Give AI one job and four weeks, then decide.","sub":"Put the date in your diary now with one question: are we still using it for this?"}
```

## 6. Fail list

| # | Failure | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Assumes kit the reader doesn't have** | Business case, pilot, rollout, seats, roadmap, stakeholders, quarterly review | Run the noun check. Replace with a job in their week: the quote, the rota, the supplier chase |
| 2 | **Platitude, not a position** | "AI is a tool, not a replacement." Nobody disagrees, nobody comments | Write the counter-argument. If there isn't one, make the take smaller and more specific until there is |
| 3 | **Makes the reader feel behind or stupid** | "If you're still not using AI in 2026..." | Aim at a habit, belief or the hype. The nervous owner should finish the post relieved |
| 4 | **Rage bait** | Picking on a group ("Gen Z can't write without ChatGPT"), "Unpopular opinion:", "Hot take:" | State the take plainly and target the habit, not people. Drop the label |
| 5 | **The card teases** | Statement card with the opinion only, an eyebrow, a URL, two empty bands | Sub line carries the usable step. No eyebrow, no URL. Text set large enough to fill the canvas |
| 6 | **The AI-post shape** | Eight one-line paragraphs, a triple of fragments, "It's not X, it's Y" | Paragraphs of one to three sentences, varied. No more than two one-liners in a row |
| 7 | **Ends with a question or a pitch** | "Agree?", "What's your first job going to be?", a link, a mention of training | End on the step or one hard sentence. Stop. Comments come from the take, and from replying in the first hour |
| 8 | **Invented evidence or fake history** | "We've seen this at dozens of firms", "most small businesses", a made-up percentage, "we got this wrong for years" | Use "our read is" and a clearly marked example ("say you run a dental practice"). Numbers only from a source opened during the run, with date |
