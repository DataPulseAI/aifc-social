## P1. Tue 29 Sep, Profile, Educate, original count. Media: 2026-09-25-twenty-of-25.png (voice card)

I counted. 20 of our 25 prompts fail without a file.

We publish 25 prompts. I went through every one this week and sorted them by what they actually need to work.

Twenty of them are useless unless you attach something real. Your accounts. Your tender. Your notes from the call. The sheet somebody else built.

Five work from a description alone, and they are the soft ones: prepare for a difficult conversation, write interview questions, plan a first month for a new starter.

That ratio is the whole thing, and it is not what people are arguing about online.

The wording is not the bottleneck. Nobody is one clever phrase away. I have watched a plain, slightly clumsy sentence with the right file attached beat a beautifully engineered prompt with nothing attached, every single time.

Attaching is the hard part, and it is not a technical problem. It is that the real file is messy. It has three tabs nobody uses, a column called Sheet1, client names in it, and a formula somebody wrote in 2019. People tidy it first, or they use a sample instead, and then wonder why the answer is generic.

The mess is the context. The thing you are embarrassed about in that file is precisely the thing that makes the answer specific to you.

So the test for any prompt you are about to write down: does it name a file? If it does not, it is advice, not a prompt, and it will work about as well as advice usually does.

## P2. Tue 29 Sep, Page, Educate, show-how. Media: 2026-09-30-two-quotes.png (prompt card)

Two quotes that look the same.

You have two proposals for the same service. Different page counts, different headings, one has a table and one does not. Most people pick the one that is easier to read, and the part that costs them turns up in year two.

Give the assistant your company already pays for both documents, exactly as they were sent, and type this:

"Compare these two proposals in a table: first-year cost, exclusions, payment terms, annual uplift, minimum term, notice period, exit costs, liability cap. Quote the clause for each. Where one is silent, write not stated."

Two clauses do the work. Quote the clause for each: you are not trusting a table, you are being told where to look. Where one is silent, write not stated: silence in a proposal is not neutral. Uplift that is not capped. Notice that is not defined. Exit costs nobody mentioned.

What good looks like: eight rows, two columns, a quoted clause in every cell or the words not stated. The not stated cells are the point. That is where the follow-up email writes itself.

What goes wrong first time: it summarises instead of quoting, because that is what it was built to do. Say quote the clause again and it will. Then read those clauses yourself. This tells you where to look. It does not tell you what to sign.

Works the same in Copilot, ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude. Nothing to install.

## P4. Thu 1 Oct, Profile, Sell, the resource. Media: licence-audit.png (numbered list card). Link: aiforcompanies.co.uk/resources/licence-audit (last line, UTM). Dependency: page live.

I keep asking owners the same question and nobody can answer it.

How many of your AI seats were opened last month?

Not how many you pay for. How many were opened.

Every vendor already tells you. Copilot, ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude all give an admin a per-seat report with last active date and active days.

So we wrote the hour that turns the report into a decision:

1. One row per seat. Name, tool, last active, active days in 28.
2. Three bands, decided before you see the names. Never, Tried, Uses.
3. One number for the board. Seats used over seats paid, as a fraction, monthly.

Then ask the Uses band one question: what do you use it for most? Three answers will be the same task. That is your team library, and nobody had to invent it.

And talk to the bottom three before you talk to any vendor. Ask what they tried, what happened and why they stopped. A second product will not fix a first one nobody was shown.

## P6. Tue 6 Oct, Profile, Educate, framework. Media: asked-vs-answered.png (comparison sheet)

Most bad AI answers are bad questions.

I have watched people decide these tools are useless on the strength of an answer they asked for in four words. Same tool, same document, thirty seconds apart, and the only thing that changes is what you typed.

Six pairs. The right-hand side of each is a line you can paste today.

The summary. Not "summarise this document". Try: summarise this for a finance director with five minutes, keep every figure and date exactly as written.

The meeting. Not "what were the actions". Try: list every action as a table, owner, action in their own words, date or trigger, and mark any action where no owner was named. That last clause is the one that earns its place. It finds the work nobody has picked up.

The contract. Not "is there anything risky in here". Try: give me the ten questions a careful buyer should ask before signing, hardest first, quoting the clause each one comes from. A summary tells you what a document says. Ten hard questions tell you what it costs you.

The rewrite. Not "make this shorter". Try: cut this to 200 words for someone who has not seen it, then list anything you removed that they might still need. The second half is the safety net, and it is the half everyone leaves off.

The gaps. Not "fill in the rest as best you can". Try: where my notes do not cover a heading, write [GAP: what is missing] rather than writing something plausible. Asked to mark its guesses, it marks them. Not asked, it does not.

The file. Not a tidied description of the spreadsheet. The actual spreadsheet, mess and all. The version you clean up first is already an interpretation, and that is where most wrong answers start.

None of that is clever. It is the difference between an answer you rewrite and one you send.

Open edge: which of the six you still type the left-hand way.

## P7. Tue 6 Oct, Page, Educate, show-how. Media: 2026-10-06-the-thread.png (prompt card)

The thread you were copied into, in 120 words.

Forty replies, three of them relevant, and somewhere in there is a decision someone is waiting on you for.

The prompt, in full:

"Summarise this thread for me as the person who has just been copied in. Give me the decision being asked for, who is waiting on whom, any dates, and the one thing I need to do. Under 120 words."

It does not ask for a summary of the thread. It asks for four named things, and the fourth is the point. The decision being asked for, not the topic. Who is waiting on whom, which is what a thread hides best. Any dates, including the ones mentioned in passing halfway down. And the one thing you need to do, because a summary of a thread is still a thread and a sentence telling you what to do is an answer.

Forward the whole message, quoted replies and all. The mess at the bottom is what tells it who was speaking to whom.

## P12. Tue 13 Oct, Page, Educate, show-how. Media: 2026-10-07-the-actions.png (prompt card)

The meeting nobody wrote up.

Every meeting has an action nobody owns. It is agreed in the room, everyone nods, and it never reaches a list because no name was attached to it. Three weeks later it is the reason something is late.

The prompt, in full:

"List every action agreed, as a table: owner, action in their own words, and the date or trigger mentioned. Mark any action where no owner was named. Do not add actions that were only discussed."

Three clauses, three jobs. In their own words: a paraphrased action is already a decision you did not make. Mark any action where no owner was named: this is the one that turns a write-up into management information. Do not add actions that were only discussed: without it you cannot tell a commitment from a thought.

Do not tidy your notes first. Half sentences carry who was speaking. A cleaned-up version loses exactly that.

## P13. Wed 14 Oct, Profile, Educate, framework. Media: find-your-four.png (numbered list card, no footer link)

Your four are already in your calendar.

Everybody has about four things they actually use these tools for. Almost nobody can say what their four are, so they keep shopping for tools instead of getting better at the ones they have.

Here is the twenty minutes that fixes it. Do it once, on last week.

Start from your own week, not a list of use cases. Open last week's calendar and your sent items and nothing else. List every task over twenty minutes that produced a document or a decision. You will probably get fifteen or twenty rows.

Cross off anything with no file behind it. If the input only ever existed in your head, it is not ready to hand over. Keep the rows where you could name the file you would attach. This usually halves the list.

Circle what you did more than once. Frequency beats difficulty. A ten-minute job you do daily is worth more than an afternoon you spend twice a year, and it is the one you will build the habit on.

Write each survivor as a prompt, once. Name who is reading it. Say what to do where the file runs out. Say what to attach. Three lines, not three paragraphs. Keep them in one note, titled by the job rather than by the tool, so the note survives you changing tools.

Review it in a month and delete what you never opened. A list that gets shorter and sharper beats a list that grows. Only add a fifth when it beats something already on it.

What you have at the end is one note, four prompts, in your own words. It is the first thing about your job that has ever been written down properly, and the next person in your role can pick it up on day one.

Not more tools. A shorter list you keep sharpening.

Open edge: how many rows you got on step one. Forty means the calendar is the problem, not the tools.

### P16 fallback: The second answer is the one that matters.

Almost every person who has told me these tools do not work described the same thing to me: they asked once, read what came back, decided it was mediocre, and closed the tab. Which is fair. The first answer usually is mediocre.

The first answer is not the product. It is the tool showing you what it understood. Read it that way and it becomes useful immediately, because now you can see exactly which part of your question it got wrong.

Too general means you did not say who it was for. Confidently wrong on detail means you described the file instead of attaching it. Missing the point entirely usually means you asked for the wrong artefact, a summary when what you wanted was a list of problems.

So say the one sentence back. Not the whole prompt again. "Same thing, but for someone who has never seen this project." "Same thing, but keep every figure exactly." "Same thing, but as questions, not a summary."

It takes ten seconds and it is the entire difference between the people who get something out of this and the people who do not.

## P17. Tue 20 Oct, Page, Educate, show-how. Media: 2026-10-09-the-update.png (prompt card)

The client update, in 200 words.

Last thing on the list, four on a Friday, and it is the single piece of writing that most shapes what a client thinks of you all week.

The prompt, in full:

"Write this week's update for [client] from the attached notes. Three headings: What moved. What is blocked. What we need from you. Then one line on next week. Under 200 words. Keep every number exactly; where the notes are unclear, write [CHECK]."

The third heading is where most updates fail. They report progress and ask for nothing. The client feels vaguely informed and does nothing, and in a fortnight you are the one chasing. Naming what you need turns an update into a piece of work with their name on it.

Under 200 words, because nobody reads the fourth paragraph of a status email. And [CHECK] wherever your own notes were unclear, so you find out what you do not know on Friday afternoon rather than in Monday's call.

Attach the week's notes in whatever state they are in. Bullet fragments are fine. That is what notes look like.

Open edge: which of the three headings your updates usually miss.
