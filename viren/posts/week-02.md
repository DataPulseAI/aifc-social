## Monday 29 September. The comparison sheet.

**Asset** `out/asked-vs-answered.png` · **Format** comparison sheet
**Mechanism** the side-by-side pair. 75 reposts against 83 comments on the reference, the
highest repost ratio in the library, because forwarding a comparison makes the sender's point
for them.
**Intention** be the thing a manager forwards to their team without writing a covering note.

> Most bad AI answers are bad questions.
>
> I have watched people decide these tools are useless on the strength of an answer they asked for in four words. Same tool, same document, thirty seconds apart, and the only thing that changes is what you typed.
>
> Six pairs. The right-hand side of each is a line you can paste today.
>
> **The summary.** Not "summarise this document". Try: summarise this for a finance director with five minutes, keep every figure and date exactly as written.
>
> **The meeting.** Not "what were the actions". Try: list every action as a table, owner, action in their own words, date or trigger, and mark any action where no owner was named. That last clause is the one that earns its place. It finds the work nobody has picked up.
>
> **The contract.** Not "is there anything risky in here". Try: give me the ten questions a careful buyer should ask before signing, hardest first, quoting the clause each one comes from. A summary tells you what a document says. Ten hard questions tell you what it costs you.
>
> **The rewrite.** Not "make this shorter". Try: cut this to 200 words for someone who has not seen it, then list anything you removed that they might still need. The second half is the safety net, and it is the half everyone leaves off.
>
> **The gaps.** Not "fill in the rest as best you can". Try: where my notes do not cover a heading, write [GAP: what is missing] rather than writing something plausible. Asked to mark its guesses, it marks them. Not asked, it does not.
>
> **The file.** Not a tidied description of the spreadsheet. The actual spreadsheet, mess and all. The version you clean up first is already an interpretation, and that is where most wrong answers start.
>
> None of that is clever. It is the difference between an answer you rewrite and one you send.
>
> All twenty-five are written out at aiforcompanies.co.uk/prompts. Free, no email.

Hook 38 characters, statement. Body 2,050 characters. No question close, no hashtags.

## Tuesday 30 September. One prompt, given whole.

**Asset** `out/2026-09-30-two-quotes.png` · **Format** prompt card
**Mechanism** one artefact, no commentary. The cheapest post to make well and the one most
reliably saved rather than liked.
**Intention** somebody uses this before Friday.

> Two quotes. Same service. Forty pages between them.
>
> This is the job nobody puts in the diary and everybody does badly, usually at five o'clock on the day it is due. You read both, you compare the headline numbers, you pick one, and the part that costs you turns up in year two.
>
> The prompt is in the image. Attach both proposals exactly as they were sent to you, not your own summary of them, and it comes back as a table:
>
> **First-year cost, exclusions, payment terms, annual uplift, minimum term, notice period, exit costs, liability cap.** The clause quoted against each one, so you can check it rather than trust it. And where a proposal says nothing at all on a line, it writes not stated.
>
> That last instruction is the whole thing. Silence in a proposal is not neutral. Uplift that is not capped, notice that is not defined, exit costs nobody mentioned. Without that line you get a tidy table that quietly hides every gap.
>
> Read the table, then go and read those clauses yourself. This tells you where to look. It does not tell you what to sign.
>
> Twenty-four more like it at aiforcompanies.co.uk/prompts.

Hook 47 characters, statement. Body 1,240 characters.
Not legal advice; the closing paragraph carries that without saying the words.

## Wednesday 1 October. The carousel.

**Asset** `carousels/2026-10-01-seven-habits.json`, nine slides · **Format** hybrid deck, a
photograph cover then designed slides
**Mechanism** taken from the twenty-four-page writing carousel: the face earns the stop, the
designed slides keep every rule legible.
**Intention** the profile reads as a person for one post rather than a publisher.
**Blocked** on one photograph. `assets/photos/01-desk-morning.jpg`, wherever the work actually
starts, not staged, phone camera, portrait. Until it is there the cover renders a dashed panel
and nothing ships.

> Seven habits, none of them clever.
>
> I am not interested in prompt engineering as a subject. I am interested in why two people with the same tool and the same document get answers half an hour apart in quality. It is almost never the tool. It is seven small things, and none of them takes any longer than the version that does not work.
>
> **Attach the real file, not a description of it.** The tidied version you paste in is already an interpretation. Give it the messy original and the mess becomes information.
>
> **Name the reader before you name the task.** "For a finance director with five minutes" changes the answer more than any clever wording you add afterwards.
>
> **Say what to do when the answer is not in there.** Ask for [GAP] or [CHECK] where your notes run out. Asked to mark its guesses, it marks them.
>
> **Ask for the questions, not the summary.** A summary tells you what a document says. Ten hard questions tell you what it costs you.
>
> **Say out loud that the numbers must not move.** Keep every figure and date exactly as written. One sentence, and it removes the failure that actually matters.
>
> **Hand it the template you already use.** Your headings, your order. Starting from your own structure beats any first draft written from nothing.
>
> **Read the first answer as a draft, not a verdict.** The second attempt is where the work is. Almost everyone who tells me these tools are useless stopped after one.
>
> All seven in the carousel, one a slide.

Hook 33 characters. Body 1,790 characters. Seven bold lead-ins, every one giving the words
rather than the advice.

## Thursday 2 October. The position.

**Asset** `out/2026-10-02-written-down.png` · **Format** voice card
**Mechanism** a claim he will defend in the comments. Contrarian openers run at 2.31% against
2.16% for a question, and this one contradicts the thing the reader has been told all year.
**Intention** the one post this week that is an argument rather than a giveaway.

> Nobody is behind on AI.
>
> That word gets used to sell things and I think it is the wrong diagnosis. The companies I see struggling with these tools are not slow. They are undocumented.
>
> Almost nothing about how a company actually works has ever been written down. How we price an unusual job. What we always check before a proposal goes out. The three things that make a good handover. Which numbers we trust and which we quietly recalculate. All of it lives in four people's heads, and two of them are on holiday.
>
> A tool cannot copy a way of doing things that has never been said out loud. So it produces something generic, and everyone concludes the technology is not there yet. The technology was never the missing part.
>
> The companies getting real value out of this did one boring thing first. They wrote down how they do the job. Not a policy document. The actual steps, in their own words, in the order they happen.
>
> That is a fortnight of unglamorous work and it pays for itself whether or not you ever open one of these tools again.

Hook 26 characters, contrarian. Body 1,180 characters.
The last two paragraphs are the reason this is not a complaint post: it names the work and the
work is free.

## Friday 3 October. No image.

**Format** text only. 36 of the hundred curated posts were text and they hold their own.
**Mechanism** the small craft observation, told at the length it deserves and no longer.
**Intention** end the week sounding like a person, not a feed.

> The second answer is the one that matters.
>
> Almost every person who has told me these tools do not work described the same thing to me: they asked once, read what came back, decided it was mediocre, and closed the tab. Which is fair. The first answer usually is mediocre.
>
> The first answer is not the product. It is the tool showing you what it understood. Read it that way and it becomes useful immediately, because now you can see exactly which part of your question it got wrong.
>
> Too general means you did not say who it was for. Confidently wrong on detail means you described the file instead of attaching it. Missing the point entirely usually means you asked for the wrong artefact, a summary when what you wanted was a list of problems.
>
> So say the one sentence back. Not the whole prompt again. "Same thing, but for someone who has never seen this project." "Same thing, but keep every figure exactly." "Same thing, but as questions, not a summary."
>
> It takes ten seconds and it is the entire difference between the people who get something out of this and the people who do not.

Hook 42 characters, statement. Body 1,190 characters. No link at all, once a week.
