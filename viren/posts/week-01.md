# Week 1: five posts, 22 to 26 September 2026

Built from `references/winners/` (the posts that beat their own author's median) and
`references/top-content/` (a hundred posts LinkedIn curated across ten industries).

Applied from those two: statement hooks by default, hook under forty characters, bodies at
1,800 to 2,400 characters rather than the 1,400 we were writing, a bold lead-in on every list
item, no question close, and text-only used without apology. Nothing in any of the five argues
for what we sell.

## Monday 22 September. The grid.
**Format** single image, `out/25-prompts.png` · **Mechanism** grouped library with a number
(Hills 4.3x, Hassid 3.5x, the highest-multiple repeatable format in the winners set)
**Intention** be the thing someone keeps. Not one reader needs to click anything for this to
have been worth their time.

> Nobody needs another list of AI tips.
>
> So here are 25 prompts instead. One for each job that quietly eats a Tuesday, written in plain words, for whichever assistant your company already pays for. The whole map is in the image.
>
> **The tender one** pulls every requirement into a table in the buyer's own words, including the one buried on page 38, with the section and page against each so you can check it.
>
> **The spreadsheet one** explains a sheet somebody else built. What is an input, what is calculated, what the totals depend on, and every place it quietly does not add up. It changes nothing, it just tells you.
>
> **The complaint one** writes a reply that owns the problem and commits to something, without grovelling and without deflecting. Most complaint replies fail in one of those two directions.
>
> **The board paper one** plays a sceptical non-executive with a finance background and hands you the eight questions they will ask, in the order they will ask them, each tied to the paragraph it comes from.
>
> **The difficult conversation one** takes a role and a situation, never a name, and gives you three points in order, the two objections most likely to come back, and one opening sentence that is direct without being hostile.
>
> Two things make all of them work, and neither is clever wording.
>
> Name who is reading. "For someone who is a finance director with five minutes" changes the answer more than any prompt engineering you will ever do.
>
> Tell it what to do when the answer is not in the file. [GAP], [CHECK], [DRIVER NEEDED]. A model asked to mark its guesses will mark them, and then you know where to look.
>
> The page has the full text of each one, what to attach, and what good output looks like, because a prompt without those last two is a party trick.
>
> Free, no email, no form: aiforcompanies.co.uk/prompts

*2,060 characters. Hook 34.*

## Tuesday 23 September. One prompt, given whole.
**Format** prompt card, `out/2026-09-24-ten-questions.png` · **Mechanism** the real artefact as
the image (Hassid's prompt screenshot, 5/5 on shareability in the cross-industry set)
**Intention** one problem, solved completely, in a post short enough to read at a bus stop.

> Reading a contract properly takes ten minutes now.
>
> Not skimming it. Reading it, with the clause numbers, in a way you could defend in a meeting.
>
> The prompt is on the card. The important word in it is the second one: **do not summarise**. A summary of a contract is the least useful thing an assistant can give you, because it flattens exactly the parts that cost money. What you want is the questions.
>
> Three details that make it work.
>
> **Order by risk, not by order of appearance.** Otherwise you get ten questions about the preamble and nothing about the liability cap.
>
> **Make it quote the clause.** Anything that cannot point at a clause is the model guessing, and you will spot it immediately.
>
> **Ask what it expected and did not find.** This is the line people leave out and it is the one that earns its keep. An indemnity that is missing does not appear anywhere in the document, so nothing else in the process will catch it.
>
> Attach the actual file. Not a description of it, not a tidied version, the real one with the schedules still bolted on the back.
>
> It is not legal advice and it does not replace the person who gives you that. It changes what you walk into their office already knowing.

*1,320 characters. Hook 41. Shorter than the median on purpose: the card carries the substance.*

## Thursday 25 September. The count.
**Format** voice card, `out/2026-09-25-twenty-of-25.png` · **Mechanism** an original count
(Grandillon got 4.2x from a 69-median account by counting something himself; nobody else can
post your number)
**Intention** the highest-value gap in the whole library. This is the first one.

> I counted. 20 of our 25 prompts fail without a file.
>
> We publish 25 prompts. I went through every one this week and sorted them by what they actually need to work.
>
> Twenty of them are useless unless you attach something real. Your accounts. Your tender. Your notes from the call. The sheet somebody else built.
>
> Five work from a description alone, and they are the soft ones: prepare for a difficult conversation, write interview questions, plan a first month for a new starter.
>
> That ratio is the whole thing, and it is not what people are arguing about online.
>
> **The wording is not the bottleneck.** Nobody is one clever phrase away. I have watched a plain, slightly clumsy sentence with the right file attached beat a beautifully engineered prompt with nothing attached, every single time.
>
> **Attaching is the hard part, and it is not a technical problem.** It is that the real file is messy. It has three tabs nobody uses, a column called Sheet1, client names in it, and a formula somebody wrote in 2019. People tidy it first, or they use a sample instead, and then wonder why the answer is generic.
>
> **The mess is the context.** The thing you are embarrassed about in that file is precisely the thing that makes the answer specific to you.
>
> So the test for any prompt you are about to write down: does it name a file? If it does not, it is advice, not a prompt, and it will work about as well as advice usually does.
>
> The 25 are free, no email: aiforcompanies.co.uk/prompts

*2,010 characters. Hook 39. The count is real and checkable against the page.*

## Friday 26 September. Permission.
**Format** voice card, `out/2026-09-26-never-opened.png` · **Mechanism** permission from someone
who could plausibly claim otherwise (Hassid 2.5x and 1.9x, Heather 1.5x for admitting she has
never used Claude Code, as an AI trainer)
**Intention** take the pressure off. Nothing else.

> Most of the AI features I pay for, I have never opened.
>
> I use about four things, most weeks.
>
> Summarise a long thread so I know what is actually being asked. Turn a messy set of notes into owners and dates. Compare two documents and tell me what differs. Draft the first version of something so I am editing instead of staring.
>
> That is it. That is the whole list.
>
> There are agents I have not built, voice modes I have not enabled, integrations I set up once and never opened again, and an entire category of features I could not name if you asked me.
>
> I am not behind and neither are you. Four things done every week beats forty things tried once, and everybody I know who is genuinely getting value out of these tools is running a short list they are slightly bored of.
>
> The anxiety in this industry is manufactured. Not by the tools, mostly by people selling courses about the tools. The actual skill is noticing which four things map onto work you already do, and then being disciplined enough not to go shopping for a fifth.
>
> If you have found your four, you are done. Go and do the work.

*1,270 characters. Hook 54. Short on purpose: permission posts lose force when explained at length.*
