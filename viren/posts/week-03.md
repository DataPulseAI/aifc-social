# Week 3: five posts, 6 to 12 October 2026

Five prompt cards, one a day, each giving one prompt away completely. Drawn from the live
library at aiforcompanies.co.uk/prompts, so nothing here is written for the post: it already
exists and anyone can check it.

The prompt card is the cheapest post to make well and the one most reliably saved rather than
liked. A week of them is deliberate. It builds the association between the profile and a
specific useful thing, which is what the reference library says compounds.

---

## Tuesday 6 October. The thread.
**Asset** `cards/2026-10-06-the-thread.png`

> Being copied in late is its own skill.
>
> Forty replies, three of them relevant, and somewhere in there is a decision someone is waiting on you for. You read it top to bottom, you lose ten minutes, and you still are not sure what you are being asked.
>
> The prompt is on the card. What makes it work is that it does not ask for a summary. It asks for four specific things: the decision being asked for, who is waiting on whom, the dates, and the one thing you have to do.
>
> That last clause is the one that earns its place. A summary of a thread is still a thread. A sentence telling you what to do is an answer.
>
> Under 120 words, because anything longer is just the thread again.
>
> Forward the whole thing, quoted replies and all. The mess at the bottom is what tells it who was speaking to whom.
>
> Twenty-four more at aiforcompanies.co.uk/prompts. Free, no email.

## Wednesday 7 October. The actions.
**Asset** `cards/2026-10-07-the-actions.png`

> Every meeting has an action nobody owns.
>
> It is agreed in the room, everyone nods, and it does not appear on anyone's list because no name was ever attached to it. Three weeks later it is the reason something is late.
>
> The prompt is on the card. It gives you a table: owner, the action in their own words, and the date or trigger that was mentioned.
>
> Then the important line. Mark any action where no owner was named.
>
> That single instruction turns a write-up into a piece of management information. You are not reading a summary of what was said, you are looking at a list of the work nobody has picked up, which is the only part of the meeting that was ever going to hurt you.
>
> One more thing: do not tidy your notes first. Half sentences and fragments carry who was speaking. A cleaned-up version loses exactly that.
>
> The rest are at aiforcompanies.co.uk/prompts.

## Thursday 8 October. The spreadsheet.
**Asset** `cards/2026-10-08-the-spreadsheet.png`

> Somebody else built the model you rely on.
>
> They have left, or they are on holiday, or it was 2019 and nobody remembers. It works, mostly, and everybody is slightly afraid of it.
>
> The prompt is on the card and it does two things. First it explains the sheet to someone who did not build it: what each column means, which cells are inputs, which are calculated. Then it lists what is inconsistent. Mixed formats, totals that do not match the range they are summing, numbers typed straight into a formula.
>
> And it changes nothing. That instruction is deliberate. You do not want a tidied version, you want to know what you are standing on.
>
> Attach the whole workbook, every tab. The tab nobody uses is usually where the problem is.
>
> This one takes about ninety seconds and I have never seen it come back empty.

## Friday 9 October. The update.
**Asset** `cards/2026-10-09-the-update.png`

> The client update you write at four on a Friday.
>
> It is the last thing on the list, you are tired, and it is the single piece of writing that most shapes what a client thinks of you all week.
>
> The prompt is on the card. Three headings, and they are the three that matter: what moved, what is blocked, what we need from you.
>
> Most updates fail on the third one. They report progress and ask for nothing, so the client reads it, feels vaguely informed, and does not act. Naming what you need turns an update into a piece of work.
>
> Two constraints do the rest. Under 200 words, because nobody reads the fourth paragraph. And keep every number exactly, with [CHECK] wherever your own notes were unclear, so you find out what you do not actually know before your client does.
>
> Attach the week's notes in whatever state they are in.
>
> aiforcompanies.co.uk/prompts

## Monday 12 October. The board paper.
**Asset** `cards/2026-10-12-the-board-paper.png`

> Read your own paper as the person who will pick it apart.
>
> Everybody proofreads. Almost nobody stress-tests, because after three drafts you cannot see it any more.
>
> The prompt is on the card. It plays a sceptical non-executive with a finance background and hands you the eight questions they would ask, in order, each tied to the paragraph it comes from.
>
> Then the second half, which is the part I would not skip. Find any claim without a number. Any risk without a mitigation. Any recommendation whose cost is not stated.
>
> Those three are the three things that get a paper sent back, and they are structural rather than stylistic, so no amount of rereading catches them.
>
> You will not agree with all eight questions. That is fine. The value is being asked them on Thursday rather than on Tuesday in the room.
>
