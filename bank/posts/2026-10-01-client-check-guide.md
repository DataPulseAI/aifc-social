Before an AI-drafted reply goes to a customer, three things get checked and everything else does not.

Most firms land on one of two rules, and both are wrong. Either everything gets read line by line, which costs more than the drafting saved, or nothing does, which works until the day it does not.

The version that survives contact with a busy week is narrow. Check anything that is a fact about the customer: their name, their order, their dates, their amounts. Check anything that is a promise: a delivery, a refund, a commitment to do something by a date. Check anything that is a rule: what your terms say, what you are allowed to offer.

Everything else is prose. If a sentence is slightly stiff, that is a cost you can bear, and it is the cost every template email has always carried anyway.

The reason to write it down as three categories rather than "use your judgement" is that judgement takes attention, and the whole point of the assistant was to free some.

For a retailer this matters most on the replies nobody senior sees: the returns, the delays, the where-is-my-order. That is where a made-up date becomes a complaint.

One more, and it is the one people skip. Whoever presses send owns what it says. That was true before any of this and it is the sentence that makes the other three stick.
