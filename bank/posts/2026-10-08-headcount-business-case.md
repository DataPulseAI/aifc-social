If your AI business case only works by cutting people, it is not an AI business case. It is a redundancy plan wearing a new badge.

We say this because of what happens next, not because of how it sounds. A project justified on headcount has to be delivered by the people it threatens. They work out the shape of it in about a fortnight, usually before the second session, and from that point you are asking them to train their replacement on a Tuesday afternoon. Adoption does not stall because the tool is difficult. It stalls because cooperating is against their interest and everyone can see it.

The second problem is that the saving rarely arrives in the form the case promised. What AI reliably removes is parts of jobs, not whole ones: the first draft, the summary, the reformatting, the chase-up email. Take 20% off forty jobs and you have not saved eight salaries. You have forty people with more time, and no plan for it.

That spare capacity is the actual return, and it is the line almost nobody writes down. What would your team do with four hours a week they do not currently have? If the answer is nothing in particular, the project has no benefit case at all, and the headcount number was standing in for the thinking.

What we would do instead is write the case around the work rather than the wage bill. Name the three tasks that should get faster. Say how you will know in a month. Then say, in one sentence, what the time released is for, and be willing to defend that sentence to the people who will release it.

A case built that way survives contact with the team doing the work. A case built on headcount gets quietly beaten by the people it was written about, and they are usually better at it than the people who wrote it.
