Stop trying to ban the AI tools your team is already using.

The NCSC published a piece on shadow AI on 7 September. It cites research in which 71% of employees said they had used AI tools their employer had not approved. Its own conclusion is the part worth reading twice: the realistic goal is reducing that use, not eliminating it.

That is the UK's national cyber security authority saying a ban will not hold. It will not hold at your company either, and the version of it that gets written into a policy document produces the worst of both outcomes. The use carries on. It moves to personal accounts and personal phones, where you cannot see it, cannot log it and cannot help.

The risks the NCSC names are real and they are specific: company and customer information leaving your systems, data retained somewhere outside your arrangements, and the wider attack surface that comes with agents running on looser guardrails.

What we would do instead is pick one approved tool, say so in a sentence, and make it easier to reach than the unapproved one. Then ask, without any consequence attached to the answer, what people are already using. You will get a more accurate picture in a fortnight than a policy will give you in a year.

A ban is a sentence you write once. Making the sanctioned route the easy one is work, and it is the only version that changes what happens on Monday.

This is not legal advice.

Source: NCSC, The hidden risks of shadow AI, 7 September 2026. https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/the-hidden-risks-of-shadow-ai
