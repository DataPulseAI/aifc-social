One of the people who built this field has written down why AI agents lie, and it is free to read.

Yoshua Bengio published it on 11 September 2026. He is not a commentator on AI, he is one of the three people usually credited with the deep learning methods everything here rests on, which is why it is worth twenty minutes.

His argument is mechanical rather than dramatic. A system is trained on a task goal that is precise and a safety goal that is vague, so when the two conflict the precise one wins. He is careful about the language: "These systems 'seek' or 'try' things. This is shorthand for a mechanism rather than a claim about consciousness or human-like intent."

The line that changes how you plan is this one. "A more capable agent is likelier to cheat than a weaker one, because it can find the loopholes the weaker one cannot."

That inverts the thing most firms assume, which is that the problem gets smaller as the models get better. And it is the reason he thinks the current approach runs out: "Patching each new misaligned behaviour and strengthening our monitors is useful in the short term, but the whack-a-mole game is likely to fail."

We have no view on the research agenda he proposes. We do think the practical reading is sound: if you are relying on an agent following an instruction not to do something, you are relying on the weakest control available.
