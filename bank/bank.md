## 1. Only 18% of UK businesses use a large language model

Only 18% of UK businesses use a large language model.

That is the ONS figure, published 20 July, for businesses with 10 or more employees. Not 18% of small firms, or 18% of some laggard sector. The most used AI technology in the country, at 18%.

The wider number gets quoted more and sounds better. Self-reported AI use has gone from around 12% to around 35% since late 2023. Nearly tripled in under three years.

But the ONS also tracked how many AI technologies each adopting business uses, and that went from around 1.4 to around 1.6 over the same period.

Adoption tripled. Depth moved by two tenths.

The rest of the breakdown is short. Visual content creation 16%, data processing with machine learning 12%, image processing 6%, robotics 2%.

Two things follow if you run a company.

You are probably not behind. If you have bought some seats and your team uses them for writing, you are doing what the median UK business is doing.

And the gap that matters is not between you and the firms that have adopted AI. It is between one use and several, and almost nobody has crossed it. The second use is harder than the first, because the first arrives on its own. People work out that it writes. Nobody works out on their own that it can reconcile two spreadsheets or draft a tender response. Someone has to show them, on their own work.

Source: ONS, Artificial intelligence in UK businesses, 2023 to 2026, 20 July 2026.

Which one thing is your team using it for?

## 2. 80% say AI made them personally more productive. 37% can point to profit.

80% say AI made them personally more productive. 37% of companies can point to any profit impact.

Both numbers are from the same McKinsey survey, published 25 August, of 1,719 people.

Eighty per cent report AI has improved their individual productivity. Half say it helps them make better decisions. Then 37% attribute at least some EBIT impact to AI, which McKinsey notes is about the same share as last year.

And the share of genuine high performers, defined as attributing at least 5% of EBIT to AI, has stayed flat at about 6%.

So the personal experience of AI is nearly universal among people using it, and the financial evidence is stuck.

The temptation is to read that as AI not working. The more careful reading is that individual time saved does not become company money unless something else changes: the work is reorganised, or the saved hours get pointed at something, or a process is actually retired.

Time saved that nobody reallocates is just a shorter afternoon.

Source: McKinsey, The state of AI in 2026: On the road to ROI, 25 August 2026.

> Sources: McKinsey 25/08/26. Tags: office, work, productivity, data

## 3. Seven in ten employees use AI tools their employer never approved

Seven in ten employees use AI tools their employer never approved.

The NCSC put that figure in a blog post on 7 September, on what it calls shadow AI. The number is 71%.

The interesting part is the advice that follows it. The NCSC does not tell organisations to lock everything down. It says the opposite: blocking AI "can mean employees turn to using AI tools that have not been approved by their organisation, introducing new cyber security risks that can be hard to identify."

Their line is that the goal "should be to reduce risk rather than assume it can be eliminated", and that a positive security culture means employees "are much less likely to turn to shadow IT services, including shadow AI".

Which is a striking thing for the national cyber security authority to say, and worth quoting at the next person who suggests a blanket ban.

If seven in ten of your people are already doing this, you do not have a policy question. You have a question about which tools you would rather they used, and whether anyone has told them.

Source: NCSC, The hidden risks of shadow AI, 7 September 2026. Not legal advice.

## 4. Only one in five UK small businesses uses AI regularly

Only one in five UK small businesses uses AI regularly. Six per cent have it built into daily work.

Enterprise Nation surveyed 1,320 UK micro and small businesses with Google, Sage, Dell and Square, published 9 June. 21% use AI regularly. Of those, 15% use it regularly for specific tasks and 6% have it built into daily work across the business.

The barriers they named, in order:

Cost of software and setting it up, 53%. Lack of skills, 46%. Data protection or privacy concerns, 38%. Lack of time to learn and set up tools, 37%. Cyber security risk, 33%. Uncertainty about whether it pays off, 32%. Lack of trusted advice, 30%.

Look at that list again. Only the first is money. The next six are all versions of the same thing: nobody has shown them.

Skills, time, whether it pays off, and who to trust are not solved by better software. They are solved by somebody sitting with a team for a day.

Source: Enterprise Nation Tech Hub, Digital and AI adoption among UK SMEs, 9 June 2026.

## 5. 88% of organisations now use AI. 6% can show it in profit.

88% of organisations now use AI. About 6% are getting real money out of it.

The first figure is Stanford's AI Index, 2026 edition: organisational adoption up to 88% of surveyed organisations. The second is McKinsey, August: the share of high performers attributing at least 5% of EBIT to AI has stayed flat at about 6%.

Different surveys, different samples, so this is not a like-for-like subtraction. But the shape is consistent across both, and it is the single most important fact about AI at work right now.

Adoption is close to universal. Returns are concentrated in a very small group.

The useful question is not whether to adopt. That is settled and everybody has. It is what the 6% do differently, and the honest answer from both reports is that they change how work is organised rather than just buying access to a model.

Sources: Stanford HAI AI Index 2026, published April 2026. McKinsey, The state of AI in 2026, 25 August 2026.

> Sources: Stanford HAI 04/26, McKinsey 25/08/26. Tags: data, work, adoption, office

## 6. Microsoft shipped 19 Copilot changes in one fortnight

Microsoft shipped 19 Copilot changes in a single fortnight.

That is the batch dated 25 August in the Microsoft 365 Copilot release notes. Nineteen entries, counted.

Most are administrative. A handful change what your team can actually do:

Copilot can now use Python when editing in Excel, which moves it from suggesting a formula to doing the analysis.

Copilot Notebooks got a new interface and can now hold Outlook emails and Teams meetings, so the context for one project stops resetting every morning.

And you can now reference an email when asking Copilot in PowerPoint to build a presentation.

The point is not any single feature. It is the rate. Nineteen changes a fortnight means the tool your team learned in January is not the tool they are using now, and nobody sends a memo when the ground shifts.

This is why one training session does not hold. Whatever you teach has a shelf life measured in months, and the thing worth teaching is how to notice what changed.

Source: Microsoft 365 Copilot release notes, batch dated 25 August 2026.

## 7. Only 26% of AI users say their leadership agrees on AI

Only 26% of AI users say their leadership is clearly and consistently aligned on AI.

Microsoft's Work Trend Index, published 5 May, surveyed 20,000 workers using AI across ten countries including the UK. One in four says the people running their organisation are on the same page about it.

The same report found 66% say AI lets them spend more time on high-value work, and 58% say they are producing work they could not have produced a year ago.

So the people doing the work can feel it. The people directing the work have not agreed what it is for.

That gap shows up elsewhere in the data. 81% of leaders say they feel safe suggesting new ways of working, against 67% of employees. 78% of leaders say managers create space to experiment, against 59% of employees. On whether reinvention is actually rewarded, 21% against 10%.

Every one of those is leaders describing a more permissive organisation than the people inside it experience.

Worth asking your own team which number they would give.

Source: Microsoft Work Trend Index 2026, Agents, human agency, and the opportunity for every organization, 5 May 2026. Base is AI users surveyed, not all employees.

> Sources: Microsoft WTI 05/05/26. Tags: leadership, work, office, team, management

## 8. Almost three-fifths of UK tech firms use AI. In construction it is 13%.

Almost three-fifths of UK information and communication businesses use AI. In construction it is 13%.

Both from the ONS release on 20 July.

The easy reading is that construction is behind. The more interesting reading is that construction has the same administrative load as everyone else, and almost none of it has been touched.

Someone in that 13% is writing method statements, chasing subcontractor paperwork, producing RAMS documents, pricing variations and answering the same tender questions they answered last quarter. All of that is text, and text is the one thing the technology is unambiguously good at.

The sectors at the top of that table did not get there because the work suited AI better. They got there because the people doing the work were already comfortable trying software on it.

That is a training gap, not a suitability gap, and it is the cheapest one in the country to close.

Source: ONS, Artificial intelligence in UK businesses, 2023 to 2026, 20 July 2026. The ONS text says "almost three-fifths" for information and communication.

## 9. Large companies are pulling away on AI agents. Smaller ones have stalled.

Large companies are pulling away on AI agents. Smaller ones have not moved at all.

McKinsey's August survey: 40% of respondents at organisations with more than $1bn revenue report scaling AI agents, up from 27% a year ago. At smaller organisations the share reporting scaling stayed flat at 22%.

One group moved thirteen points. The other moved nothing.

The same pattern holds for AI generally. 54% of respondents at billion-dollar organisations report scaling AI across the enterprise, against roughly a third at smaller ones.

It would be easy to explain this with budget. Agents are not especially expensive, though. What large organisations have is somebody whose job is to work out where a new capability fits, test it, and roll it out. Smaller organisations have people who are already fully occupied doing the actual work.

That is the real asymmetry, and it is worth naming, because "we do not have the budget" is a different problem from "we do not have anyone with the time".

Source: McKinsey, The state of AI in 2026: On the road to ROI, 25 August 2026.

> Sources: McKinsey 25/08/26. Tags: enterprise, sme, agents, work, data

## 10. Generative AI hit 53% adoption faster than the internet did

Generative AI reached 53% adoption in three years. That is faster than the personal computer, and faster than the internet.

Stanford's AI Index 2026 puts it plainly, alongside the finding that generative AI is now used in at least one business function at 70% of organisations.

The comparison is genuinely startling, and it is also the reason so many rollouts feel chaotic.

The PC took about a decade to reach that level of use. That decade was not wasted time. It was when organisations worked out job titles, training, file storage conventions, who fixes it when it breaks and what you are allowed to put on it.

Generative AI has had three years, and most organisations have not done any of that. The tool arrived before the habits did.

The AI Index's own framing of the year is "a widening gap between what AI can do and how prepared we are to manage it". That gap is not technical.

Source: Stanford HAI, AI Index Report 2026, published April 2026.

> Sources: Stanford HAI 04/26. Tags: adoption, history, work, data, office

## 11. Nine in ten organisations use AI somewhere. Just over half use it in three places.

Nine in ten organisations use AI somewhere. Just over half use it in three or more places.

McKinsey, August: nearly nine in ten respondents report regular use of AI in at least one business function, and the share using it in three or more functions rose from 51% to 56%.

That five-point move is the number worth watching, and it is the same story the ONS tells about the UK, where the average adopting business went from around 1.4 AI technologies to around 1.6.

Two different surveys, two different measures, one finding: spreading AI to a second and third part of the business is much harder than getting it into the first.

The first function is nearly always the one where somebody was already curious. Marketing, or support, or whoever writes the most. It happens without a plan.

The second needs a plan, because the people in that function have not volunteered and do not yet see what it would do for them.

Sources: McKinsey, The state of AI in 2026, 25 August 2026. ONS, AI in UK businesses, 20 July 2026.

> Sources: McKinsey 25/08/26, ONS 20/07/26. Tags: work, adoption, office, team

## 12. Only 14% of companies say AI shrank their workforce

Only 14% of companies using AI say it contributed to any decline in headcount last year.

That is McKinsey's August survey, and it is worth holding next to the volume of commentary suggesting otherwise.

The same survey found something less comfortable. Among mid-level managers and individual contributors, 47% said they had experienced a negative effect from AI at work, against 31% of executives and senior managers.

So the jobs story and the experience story are not the same story. Headcount is mostly holding. The people lower down the organisation are having a materially worse time of it than the people above them, and by a sixteen point margin.

If you are running a rollout and taking your read on how it is going from your management team, that gap is the thing to go and check.

Ask the people who are actually using it daily, not the people who approved it.

Source: McKinsey, The state of AI in 2026: On the road to ROI, 25 August 2026.

> Sources: McKinsey 25/08/26. Tags: jobs, work, management, team, office

## 13. The UK wants to be the most AI-confident SME economy in the G7 by 2035

The UK government's stated ambition is for British SMEs to be the most digitally capable and AI confident in the G7 by 2035.

That is from the SME Digital Adoption Taskforce update published in June and updated on 14 September.

Set it against what the measurements currently say. The ONS has 28% of businesses with fewer than 10 employees using any AI technology. Enterprise Nation has 21% of small businesses using AI regularly and 6% with it embedded.

Nine years is a long time, and the gap is a training gap rather than a technology one, so it is closable.

But nothing in the current numbers is on that trajectory by itself, and the taskforce update is a progress report on delivery, not new evidence of adoption.

Worth knowing the target exists, if only so you can judge the support that gets announced against it.

Sources: GOV.UK, SME Digital Adoption Taskforce 2026 update, published 26 June 2026, updated 14 September 2026. ONS, 20 July 2026. Enterprise Nation, 9 June 2026.

## 14. The cheapest AI intervention in your business is a list

The cheapest thing you can do about AI this month costs nothing and takes an hour.

Ask everyone to write down the three tasks they do most often that are mostly text.

Not what they think AI should do. What they actually do. Writing the same email, summarising a call, filling in the same form, turning notes into a document, checking a long thing for one detail.

Two things happen. You get a list of candidates ranked by how much time they consume, which is better targeting than any vendor will give you. And people who had not connected AI to their own work suddenly have.

This matters because of what the numbers say about where organisations get stuck. The ONS has the average adopting UK business using around 1.6 AI technologies. Enterprise Nation has 46% of small businesses naming lack of skills as a barrier and 37% naming lack of time.

None of that is solved by more access. It is solved by somebody pointing at a specific task and saying, try it on this one.

The list is how you find the task.

Sources: ONS, 20 July 2026. Enterprise Nation Tech Hub, 9 June 2026.

> Sources: ONS 20/07/26, Enterprise Nation 09/06/26. Tags: work, team, practical, office

## 15. Half your AI policy can fit on one page

Most AI policies fail for a reason nobody wants to say out loud. Nobody read them.

The NCSC's shadow AI post in September found 71% of employees using AI tools their employer had not approved, and its advice was not to clamp down. Its line was that the goal "should be to reduce risk rather than assume it can be eliminated".

A policy people actually follow answers four questions, and it fits on one page.

Which tools are approved, named, so nobody has to guess.

What must never be pasted in. Client data, personal data, anything under NDA, anything you would not email to the wrong address.

Who checks the output before it goes out, and that it is always a person.

Who to ask. One name.

Everything else is elaboration. A page gets read at induction and pinned up. A twelve page document gets acknowledged and never opened again, which is the same as having no policy while feeling like you have one.

Sources: NCSC, The hidden risks of shadow AI, 7 September 2026. This is not legal advice.

> Sources: NCSC 07/09/26. Tags: policy, risk, work, uk, practical

## 16. 58% say they are producing work they could not have done a year ago

58% of AI users say they are producing work they could not have produced a year ago.

Microsoft's Work Trend Index, 5 May, 20,000 workers using AI across ten countries.

That is a bigger claim than time saved, and a more interesting one. Time saved is an efficiency story and it shows up as a slightly shorter afternoon. New work that was previously out of reach is a capability story, and it changes what a small team can credibly take on.

In practice it is the small firm that can now answer a tender it would have skipped, or produce the research pack it used to buy in, or translate its own materials.

The same survey found 66% saying AI lets them spend more time on high-value work.

Neither number tells you whether it reached the accounts, and McKinsey's data suggests mostly it has not yet. But of the two framings, capability is the one worth planning around. Efficiency gets absorbed. New capability gets sold.

Source: Microsoft Work Trend Index 2026, 5 May 2026. Base is AI users surveyed, not all employees.

> Sources: Microsoft WTI 05/05/26. Tags: work, capability, sme, team, office
