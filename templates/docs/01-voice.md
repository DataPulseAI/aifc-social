# AI for Companies: voice, on the page and on LinkedIn

Written 15 September 2026. This governs everything the content engine publishes.
If a draft breaks a rule here, the rule wins.

---

## 1. Who we are talking to

One person: a manager or owner at a UK company between roughly 10 and 250 staff.
They have bought some AI seats, or they are about to. They are not a technologist.
They are not anti-AI. They are quietly worried that they spent money on something
their team opened twice and abandoned.

They are busy, mildly sceptical, and they have read forty LinkedIn posts this week
telling them they are behind. They do not need a forty-first.

What they actually want to know, in order:

1. Is the thing I already pay for good enough?
2. What do I make people do on Monday?
3. How do I know in a month whether it worked?
4. What is the rule about our data, in one sentence I can repeat?

Write to those four questions and the content will be useful. Drift off them and it
becomes another AI hype account.

---

## 2. The stance

We are the people who say the calm thing in a loud room.

The whole market is shouting that AI changes everything and you are already losing.
Our position is narrower and more defensible: **the tools are fine, the training is
the gap.** UK adoption is climbing and depth is not. That is a training problem, and
training is what we sell. Every post should be downstream of that claim.

Three things this stance lets us do that competitors cannot:

- **Tell people not to buy things.** Recommending the assistant they already own is
  more persuasive than any pitch, and it is usually correct.
- **Be specific about failure.** We can say "most rollouts stall in week three" because
  we are selling the fix, not the tool.
- **Refuse the hype cycle.** When a model launches, we are not the account posting
  benchmark charts. We are the account asking whether it changes what your team does
  on Monday. Usually the answer is no, and saying so builds more trust than saying yes.

## 3. Voice rules, hard

These are not preferences. They are checkable.

**Punctuation and mechanics**

- No em dashes. No double hyphens. Use a comma, a full stop, or rewrite the sentence.
- UK English throughout. Organisation, programme, prioritise, analyse, whilst is
  avoided but realise and recognise take the s.
- No italics for emphasis. Emphasis comes from sentence position and word choice.
- Numerals for anything countable. Nine or fewer can be spelled out in prose.
- Dates written as 20 July 2026, never 07/20/26.
- No exclamation marks. Ever. One is a tell, two is a personality we do not have.

**Person and address**

- We are "we" and "our team". Never "I", because the company delivers this, not one
  person. Never "the practitioner" or any consultant-speak third person.
- The reader is "you" and "your team". Never "one", never "businesses" when you mean
  the person reading.
- Never "folks", "friends", "team" as a vocative, or any address that implies a crowd.

**Claims and evidence**

- Every number carries a source and a date, in the post or on the card. No exceptions.
- Never invent a client, a result, a testimonial or a quote. We have no delivery
  history to point at yet, and fabricating one is both wrong and the fastest way to
  lose the only asset we have.
- Never imply we did work we did not do. "Rollouts stall in week three" is an
  observation about the market. "Our clients' rollouts" is a lie until it is not.
- Where something is genuinely our opinion, say so in the sentence: "our read is",
  "we would".
- Anything touching UK GDPR, employment or contracts gets the line: this is not legal
  advice.

**Length and shape**

- Hook lands inside 140 characters. That is the mobile truncation point, so everything
  after it is invisible until someone taps.
- Body length by format: text-only post 1,000 characters and up, image post 800 to 900,
  carousel caption under 150. Carousel captions that run long suppress the tap.
- Paragraphs of one to three sentences, with a blank line between them.
- One idea per post. If a draft has two, it is two posts.

## 4. Words and constructions we do not use

This list exists because these are the exact fingerprints of machine-written LinkedIn
copy in 2026. A reader cannot always articulate why a post feels synthetic, but they
feel it, and they scroll. Every item below appeared in the competitor posts we
studied, which is precisely why we avoid them.

**Banned outright**

| Do not write | Because |
|---|---|
| delve, leverage (as a verb), utilise, robust, seamless, unlock, harness, elevate, supercharge, game changer, landscape, realm, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, vital | The canonical LLM vocabulary. Any one of them tags the post |
| "In today's fast paced world", "In an era where", "Let's dive in", "The bottom line" | Filler openings that say nothing |
| "It's not X, it's Y" as a standalone line | The single most overused LinkedIn rhythm of the last two years |
| "Here's the thing nobody tells you" | Promises a secret and never delivers one |
| "Most people are doing X wrong" | Every AI account opens this way now. If the insight is real, state the insight |
| "Read that again" | Nobody has ever read it again |
| "Save this before it gets buried" | Begging |
| "Genuinely curious", "honestly", "straightforward" | We are honest by default. Saying so invites doubt |
| Rocket, fire, brain, target, 100 emoji | No emoji at all, see below |
| Any hashtag block at the end | Measurably reduces reach and reads as 2019 |

**Structural tells to avoid**

- **The one line per sentence stack.** Eight consecutive single-sentence paragraphs is
  now the most recognisable AI-post shape on the platform. Vary paragraph length.
  Some paragraphs are three sentences. Some are one. That variation is the signal.
- **Arrow bullets everywhere.** The → prefix on every list item is fine occasionally
  and exhausting as a default. Prefer numbered lists when order matters and plain
  sentences when it does not.
- **The triple.** "No context. No structure. No memory." Three fragments in a row is a
  rhetorical tic that now reads as generated. Once in a long post is fine. Twice is
  a pattern.
- **Symmetrical contrast pairs.** "Amateurs do X. Professionals do Y." Useful once,
  corrosive when every post is built on it.
- **Fake vulnerability.** "I got this wrong for years." If it did not happen, do not
  write it. We are a company account and we have no personal history to confess.
- **The engagement-bait close.** "Comment YES", "Which one are you trying first?",
  "Drop it below". LinkedIn explicitly downranks this since March 2026. See section 6
  for what to do instead.

**The positive test**

Before a post ships, read the first two lines aloud. If they could sit at the top of
any of the forty AI accounts we studied with only the brand name swapped, rewrite
them. The fix is almost always the same: **replace the generic claim with the specific
thing.** Not "most rollouts fail" but "Copilot rollouts stall in week three, when the
novelty goes and nobody owns the prompt library."

## 5. How we open

The hook is the whole game and it lives inside 140 characters. Evidence from a
1.18 million post study published in June 2026: opening with a **question** costs about
34% of likes and produces fewer comments, while opening with a **number** earns more.
Hooks of one to ten words outperform longer ones.

So: open with a number, a named thing, or a flat claim. Put the question at the end
if you want one at all.

**Openings that work for us**

- The specific number with a date: "35% of UK firms now use AI. The average one uses
  it for 1.6 tasks."
- The named moment: "Week three is where Copilot rollouts die."
- The permission: "Your team is not behind. It has not been shown how."
- The refusal: "You probably do not need another AI tool."
- The concrete artefact: "Here is the one page AI policy a UK company actually needs."
- The correction: "Microsoft shipped 11 Copilot changes this fortnight. Three matter."

**Openings that do not**

- Anything beginning "Most people", "Everyone is", "Nobody talks about".
- Anything that withholds the subject to create suspense.
- Anything that opens with our own name or an announcement. Nobody followed us for news
  about us.

## 6. Closing, and how we ask for things

LinkedIn's March 2026 update downranks engagement bait, and the keyword-harvesting
close ("comment GUIDE and I will DM it") is the exact pattern it names. We do not use
it. We also do not need it, because our lead magnets are ungated at the point of the
click and gated at the point of the download, which is a better trade for everyone.

Our three closes, in rough order of how often they should appear:

1. **The resource line.** One plain sentence naming what is on the site and where.
   "The full ninety day plan is at aiforcompanies.co.uk/insights." This goes in the
   post body, not the first comment. On an image or carousel post a body link costs
   only about 5 to 15% of reach, which is a price worth paying for a click that lands.

2. **The substantive question.** Not "thoughts?" but a question only a real reader can
   answer, and one whose answers tell us something commercially. "Which of the six is
   unowned at your place?" A question that produces three paragraph replies is worth
   more than one that produces thirty one-word comments, because threaded replies carry
   up to 2.4 times the reach of a standard comment.

3. **Nothing at all.** The strongest posts in our study closed with a hard sentence and
   stopped. If the post has given something complete, asking for a comment cheapens it.
   Roughly one post in four should simply end.

Never: "repost this", "follow for more", "save this", "tag someone". The reposts we
want come from posts worth reposting.

## 7. What we sound like, in one paragraph

Plain, specific, unhurried, and slightly contrarian about whether the reader needs to
panic. We know more than we say. We give away the method because the method is not the
hard part, delivery is. We would rather be the account that told someone to keep the
tool they had than the one that sold them a fourth. When we do not know, we say the
evidence is thin, and that single habit will separate us from every other account in
this category.
