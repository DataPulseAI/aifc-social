# How we write

One document for the sound of everything we publish: profile, page, cards, comments, emails.
Written 24 September 2026. SYSTEM.md says what to publish and when. This says how it should
read. Where a draft breaks a rule here, the rule wins.

## Who is reading

A person at their desk with a licence they did not ask for. An account manager copied into a
forty-message thread. A finance lead with a spreadsheet somebody else built. An owner of a
60-person firm who has paid for Copilot and is not sure anyone opens it. They are not
technical, not anti-AI, and not behind. They have read forty posts this week telling them they
are. They do not need a forty-first.

So the reader is the person doing the task, and the owner is the buyer. Write for the first,
and the second sees their team in it. A post only an owner can act on is a selling post, and
there is one of those a fortnight. Everything else has to be usable today by someone with no
budget and no title. That is the message underneath all of it: AI is for everyone, every
company, every team.

## What it sounds like

Like a colleague who has done the thing, telling you how, in the time it takes to make a
coffee. Plain, specific, unhurried, a little contrarian about whether anyone needs to panic.
We know more than we say. We give the method away because the method is not the hard part.
We would rather tell someone to keep the tool they have than sell them a fourth. When the
evidence is thin, we say so.

On the profile, Viren speaks as himself: I, my, me. First person is the point of the profile.
On the page, the company speaks: we, our. The reader is always you.

## The four tests every line passes

**The colleague test.** Would you say this sentence to someone across a desk? "Silence is a
gap, and now you can see every one" fails. "Two have nobody's name on them" passes. If a line
sounds like a caption written about the work rather than a thing said to a person, it goes.

**The first 90 characters.** On a phone, that is all anyone sees before "more". The moment and
the outcome go there, in that order. "Two supplier quotes, one table, every gap marked. Fifteen
minutes before you sign." Start with the end. The set-up, if it survives at all, comes after.

**The card test.** The image carries the steps, the prompt or the number. The body carries the
why. Never both. If a sentence in the body restates a line on the card, cut it.

**The ask test.** Ask the question or make the statement. Never seek permission to do either.
"One question back, if you have time for it" is not how people talk. "Which process at your firm
got written down properly this year?" is. Nobody writes "I would like to be proved wrong".

## Shape

Hook, then the outcome, inside 90 characters. Body under about 1,100 characters, paragraphs of
one to three sentences with a blank line between, lengths varied so it does not read as a stack
of one-liners. One idea per post; two ideas is two posts. End on a hard sentence a reader can
dispute, or a plain instruction. Not a recap, not "so remember", and a direct question at most
twice a fortnight.

Numbers come with a source and a date, in the post or on the card, and the source was opened
before it was cited. No invented clients, results, quotes or headcounts. Illustrative examples say
"Illustrative data" and nothing more. Anything near UK GDPR, employment or contracts carries
"not legal advice".

## The comment under the post

One CTA per post, and it lives in the first comment, never in the body, except a resource post
whose whole point is the document. The comment is useful on its own: the prompt as copyable text,
the six lines as a note template, the three follow-ups to paste. Where there is a link it is one,
it carries UTM, and the sentence around it says what the reader gets: "The 25, with the file each
one needs. Free:" and the link. Or the comment asks the one question the post earned. Nothing
begs. "Save this", "follow for more", "comment X for the guide" and "tag someone" are out, and so
is asking people to comment so that they can be sent something.

## Words we do not use

Machine fingerprints: delve, leverage, utilise, robust, seamless, unlock, harness, elevate,
supercharge, game changer, landscape, realm, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, vital,
navigate, empower, journey. Filler openers: "in today's world", "let's dive in", "here's the
thing". Confidence words that invite doubt: genuinely, honestly, straightforward, truly.
Shapes: "Most people" as an opener, "It's not X, it's Y" as a line on its own, "read that
again", three fragments in a row more than once a post, the amateurs-versus-professionals pair,
eight one-sentence paragraphs in a row. Claims about the writing itself: "writes itself",
"quietly", "elegant", "beautiful". Commercial padding: "no email", "no sign-up", "nothing to
fill in". Say free and stop.

Mechanics: UK English. No em dashes, no double hyphens, no exclamation marks, no emoji, no
hashtags, no italics for emphasis. Dates as 20 July 2026. The brand is AIforCompanies, one
word, and Viren's line is "Founder, Convu & AIforCompanies".

## Before and after, from this week

Set-up before payoff. Before: "Comparing two supplier quotes usually means two PDFs open side
by side and an exit fee you did not see on page 24 until year two. Do not ask for a summary."
After: "Two supplier quotes, one table, every gap marked. Fifteen minutes before you sign, so you
ask the questions before the invoice does."

The caption that thinks it is clever. Before: "What comes back. The not stated cells are where
the follow-up email writes itself." After: "What comes back. Four cells say not stated. Those are
your four questions to the supplier before you sign."

The permission-seeking comment. Before: "One question back, if you have time for it. Which
process at your firm did someone write down properly this year, and who did it? Pricing is my bet
for the least written-down, and I would like to be proved wrong." After: "Which process at your
firm got written down properly this year? Pricing is my bet for the one that never does."

The body that repeats the card. Before: a 1,800-character post listing all six prompt pairs that
were already on the image. After: "The card has the six pairs. This is the why, so you can write
your own seventh," followed by one line of reason for each.

The banned shape. Before: "Most people I watch still type the left-hand side." After: "Almost
everyone I watch still types the left-hand side."

## The check before anything ships

Read the first 90 characters alone. Do they carry the moment and the outcome. Read the body
aloud; anything you would not say to a colleague goes. Check the card and the body do not say the
same thing. Count the CTAs; one, in the comment. Search for the banned words and the banned
shapes. Confirm every number has a source and a date and that the source was opened. Check the
byline and the brand string. If it is illustrative, does it say so. If it touches contracts,
employment or data, does it say not legal advice. Then, and only then, into Buffer as a draft with
its date, for Viren to approve.
